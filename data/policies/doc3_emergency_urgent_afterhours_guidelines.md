## Part 1 of 7 — Purpose, Scope, Governance, Definitions, and Safety Philosophy

## 1. About These Guidelines

### 1.1 Title and Identification

These are the **Emergency, Urgent & After-Hours Care Guidelines** of Lakeside Regional Medical Center (LRMC). They are policy document **EUA-150** within the LRMC Administrative Policy Library. They define how LRMC staff — and the organization's automated triage-support assistant — recognize symptoms that require emergency or urgent care, guide patients to the right level of care, operate the after-hours nurse line, and apply standardized clinical pathways for the highest-risk presentations that arrive through patient messages and calls.

LRMC is a 400-bed community teaching hospital and affiliated outpatient network at 1200 Lakeshore Parkway, Rochester, NY 14600. The hospital main line is 585-230-0000. The **after-hours nurse line is 585-230-0911**. For any emergency, the instruction throughout these guidelines is to **call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department**.

### 1.2 Why These Guidelines Exist

Most patient messages and calls describe routine matters, but a small and critical fraction describe time-sensitive emergencies — a possible heart attack, stroke, severe breathing trouble, anaphylaxis, or a mental-health crisis. In an asynchronous channel that is not monitored continuously, the difference between recognizing and missing such a message can be the difference between a good and a catastrophic outcome. These guidelines exist to:

1. **Recognize danger fast.** Give staff and the automated assistant clear, concrete red-flag lists so possible emergencies are identified immediately rather than sitting in a routine queue.
2. **Direct patients to the right level of care.** Help patients and staff distinguish when to call 911, go to the Emergency Department, use urgent care, use telehealth, or wait for a routine reply.
3. **Standardize the highest-risk pathways.** Provide consistent, safe pathways for chest pain, stroke, breathing difficulty, and mental-health crisis, where minutes matter and variation is dangerous.
4. **Make replies safe by default.** Define exactly what any reply — automated draft or staff message — must say when emergency indicators appear, so no emergency message ever receives a casual or delayed response.

### 1.3 How to Use These Guidelines

Each numbered section is written to stand on its own. If you need only the red-flag list for breathing problems, the rule for when to call 911 for chest pain, or the mandatory language for an emergency reply, you should be able to act from that single section without reading the whole document. Cross-references point to related detail, but the essential rule is always restated where it is used.

These guidelines work hand in hand with the **Patient Communication & Triage Policy (PCT-100)**, which defines the four urgency categories (Emergency, Urgent, Routine, Informational) and the overall message workflow. EUA-150 provides the clinical recognition content that supports the Emergency and Urgent categories in PCT-100.

### 1.4 A Critical Limitation — These Guidelines Do Not Replace Clinical Judgment or 911

These guidelines are decision-support tools, not a substitute for professional clinical judgment or for emergency services. No red-flag list can capture every dangerous presentation. When a patient's situation seems dangerous even though it does not match a listed red flag, staff treat it as an emergency and act accordingly. Nothing in this document instructs a patient to delay calling 911 when they believe they are having an emergency, and no automated tool is permitted to talk a patient out of seeking emergency care.

### 1.5 Relationship to Other LRMC Policies

- **PCT-100 — Patient Communication and Triage Policy.** Defines urgency categories, response-time standards, escalation, documentation, and the human-in-the-loop rule for replies. EUA-150 supplies the clinical recognition content behind PCT-100's Emergency and Urgent categories.
- **PCT-210 — Telephone Triage and Nurse Advice Line Policy.** Governs the after-hours nurse line (585-230-0911) operated under Part 4 of this document.
- **BH-600 — Behavioral Health Access and Suicide-Risk Response.** Governs the mental-health-crisis pathway in Part 5 (Behavioral Health Access Line, 585-230-3911).
- **RX-500 — Outpatient Prescription and Refill Management.** Governs medication and overdose-related considerations referenced in the pathways (Pharmacy, 585-230-2100).
- **HIM-300 — Documentation and the Legal Medical Record.** Governs documentation of emergency recognition and disposition.
- **PRIV-400 — Patient Privacy and Confidential Communications.** Governs identity verification and welfare-check considerations.

## 2. Scope and Applicability

### 2.1 What These Guidelines Cover

These guidelines apply to the **recognition of, and response to, possible emergency and urgent conditions** in patient communications and calls handled by LRMC, including:

- Portal messages (MyLRMC) screened for emergency indicators under PCT-100.
- Telephone calls to clinics and to the after-hours nurse line (585-230-0911) under PCT-210.
- The automated triage-support assistant's screening of messages for emergency content and its proposed urgency category and draft reply.
- Patient-facing guidance materials that help patients choose the right level of care.

### 2.2 What These Guidelines Do Not Cover

These guidelines do **not** govern: the internal clinical operations of the LRMC Emergency Department or the care delivered there; EMS and 911 dispatch, which LRMC does not operate; inpatient rapid-response or code protocols; or the detailed medication rules in RX-500. They guide **recognition and direction to care**, not the delivery of emergency treatment.

### 2.3 Who Must Follow These Guidelines

These guidelines apply to every LRMC workforce member who reads, triages, or answers patient messages or calls — triage nurses, providers, clinical support staff, scheduling and access staff, pharmacy staff, and the after-hours nurse line team — as well as to the configuration and oversight of the automated triage-support assistant. Only staff who have completed the required training (Part 7) handle messages or calls under these guidelines.

## 3. Safety Philosophy

### 3.1 Zero Missed Emergencies, Minimized False Alarms

The design goal of LRMC's emergency recognition is to **miss no true emergency**, while keeping false alarms as low as reasonably possible. These two goals are not equal: when they conflict, the system favors catching the emergency. A small number of patients safely directed to a higher level of care than they turned out to need is an acceptable cost of never missing a patient who truly needed it.

### 3.2 Recognition Uses Judgment, Not Keyword Matching Alone

Emergency recognition relies on clinical judgment applied to the **whole message in context**, not on isolated keyword matching. The same word can mean very different things — "chest pain right now" is an emergency, while "I had chest pain after my surgery two years ago" is a historical mention. The red-flag lists in Part 2 describe **active or recent** danger signs; staff and the automated assistant read for timing and context, so that a historical mention does not manufacture a false emergency and an active danger sign is never missed.

### 3.3 The Asynchronous Channel Is the Core Risk

Because portal messages are not monitored continuously, the central risk is the delay between a patient sending an emergency message and a human identifying it. Every control in these guidelines — patient-facing prevention, automated screening, the after-hours nurse line, and the mandatory emergency reply language — exists to shorten that delay and to direct the patient to a continuously available resource (911 or the ED) rather than rely on the portal.

### 3.4 Fail Safe

When recognition is uncertain or a tool is unavailable, the system fails toward safety: the more urgent interpretation governs, the patient is directed to a monitored channel (911, the ED, or the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911), and a human clinician reviews. The automated assistant never lowers urgency a human would assign, and never talks a patient out of emergency care; if it is unavailable, the standard human workflow handles everything with no loss of safety.

### 3.5 Respect and Plain Language

Even under urgency, communication is plain, respectful, and clear. An emergency instruction is direct ("Call 911 now") without being alarming in a way that confuses. Interpreter Services (585-230-6400) are offered to patients with limited English proficiency, and instructions are given in language a frightened patient can act on.

## 5. Definitions

Each definition stands alone so a reader can resolve it wherever the term appears.

**Emergency.** A known or possible life-threatening or limb-threatening condition, or one requiring immediate in-person evaluation, where delay risks serious harm. The response is to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department immediately.

**Red flag.** A specific symptom or sign that, when active or recent, indicates a possible emergency requiring 911 or the Emergency Department. Red-flag lists are in Part 2.

**Urgent.** A condition that is not immediately life-threatening but needs prompt attention — typically same-day — to prevent deterioration or relieve significant symptoms. Urgent conditions are often appropriate for urgent care, a same-day visit, or prompt nurse-line guidance.

**Emergency Department (ED).** The hospital-based facility for evaluating and treating emergencies, available 24/7, with capabilities (such as emergency imaging, labs, and resuscitation) that urgent care and telehealth do not have. The LRMC Emergency Department information line is 585-230-1911; patients in an emergency call 911 or go directly to the nearest ED.

**Urgent care.** A walk-in clinic for non-life-threatening problems that need prompt care but not the ED — for example, minor injuries, infections, or moderate symptoms. LRMC's Lakeside Urgent Care is reachable at 585-230-1700. Urgent care is **not** for emergencies.

**Telehealth / virtual care.** A remote visit by video or phone with a clinician, suitable for many non-urgent and some urgent issues that do not require a physical examination or testing. LRMC Virtual Care is reachable at 585-230-3500. Telehealth is **not** for emergencies.

**After-hours nurse line.** The telephone service (585-230-0911) staffed for live clinical triage outside business hours under PCT-210, where a nurse can assess a concern and direct the patient to the right level of care. It is **not** a replacement for 911.

**Time-critical condition.** A condition where treatment benefit declines rapidly with time — notably heart attack, stroke, severe breathing failure, anaphylaxis, severe bleeding, and sepsis — for which immediate 911/ED response is essential.

**Welfare check.** A request for in-person assistance to a patient who may be in danger and cannot be reached, made under clinician direction and Risk Management guidance (585-230-8100), typically in a life-threatening situation such as an unreachable patient who expressed suicidal intent.

**Mandatory emergency language.** The specific content every reply must contain when emergency indicators appear, defined in Part 6.

## Part 2 of 7 — Red-Flag Symptom Lists That Require 911 or the Emergency Department

## 6. How to Use the Red-Flag Lists

The lists below name **active or recent** symptoms and signs that require a patient to **call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department**. They are organized by body system and situation so that a staff member or the automated assistant handling a specific complaint can find the relevant red flags quickly. Three rules govern their use, restated here because each section may be read alone:

1. **Active or recent, not historical.** A red flag applies to a symptom happening now or starting recently, not to a past episode the patient is merely recounting. When timing is unclear, staff clarify and, in the interim, treat it as active.
2. **The list is not exhaustive.** If a presentation seems dangerous but is not listed, treat it as an emergency anyway. Judgment overrides the list in the protective direction.
3. **Classify upward when in doubt.** When a symptom could be a red flag, act as if it is, and let a clinician confirm or de-escalate after assessment.

---

## 7. Cardiovascular Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Treat as an emergency when a patient describes, now or recently:

- **Chest pain, pressure, tightness, or squeezing** lasting more than a few minutes or coming and going, especially with exertion.
- Chest discomfort **with** shortness of breath, sweating, nausea or vomiting, lightheadedness, or a sense of doom.
- Pain **spreading** to the arm (especially left), shoulder, neck, jaw, or back.
- **Sudden severe palpitations** with fainting, near-fainting, chest pain, or shortness of breath.
- **Fainting (syncope)** or near-fainting, especially with exertion, chest pain, or palpitations.
- Signs of poor circulation to a limb — a **cold, pale, painful, or numb leg or arm** of sudden onset.

These can indicate a heart attack, dangerous arrhythmia, or vascular emergency. The chest-pain pathway is detailed in Part 5.

---

## 8. Neurologic Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Treat as an emergency when a patient describes, now or recently, any sign of stroke or serious neurologic event. The **BE-FAST** signs are emphasized:

- **B — Balance:** sudden loss of balance, coordination, or dizziness with other signs.
- **E — Eyes:** sudden vision loss, double vision, or loss of vision in one eye.
- **F — Face:** sudden facial drooping or numbness, often on one side.
- **A — Arms:** sudden weakness or numbness of an arm or leg, often on one side.
- **S — Speech:** sudden slurred speech, trouble speaking, or trouble understanding.
- **T — Time:** any of the above is time-critical — call 911 immediately and note when symptoms started.

Also treat as an emergency:

- **Sudden, severe headache** ("the worst headache of my life"), especially with stiff neck, vomiting, confusion, or fainting.
- **Seizure**, especially a first seizure, a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, repeated seizures, or failure to return to normal afterward.
- **Sudden confusion, difficulty staying awake, or unresponsiveness.**
- **Sudden severe weakness or numbness** anywhere of unclear cause.

The stroke pathway is detailed in Part 5.

---

## 9. Respiratory Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Treat as an emergency when a patient describes, now or recently:

- **Severe shortness of breath** or difficulty breathing **at rest**, or struggling to speak in full sentences because of breathlessness.
- **Bluish lips or face**, or a feeling of suffocation.
- **Choking** or an obstructed airway.
- A **severe asthma or COPD attack** not relieved by usual rescue treatment.
- **Coughing up blood** in more than small streaks, or severe.
- Sudden shortness of breath **with chest pain or with a swollen, painful leg** — possible blood clot in the lung.

The breathing-difficulty pathway is detailed in Part 5.

---

## 10. Allergic / Anaphylaxis Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Treat as an emergency — possible anaphylaxis — when a patient describes, now or recently:

- **Swelling of the lips, tongue, throat, or face**, or a tight throat or trouble swallowing.
- **Trouble breathing, wheezing, or a hoarse voice** after a likely trigger (food, sting, medication).
- **Widespread hives** with faintness, vomiting, or a sense of doom.
- A known severe-allergy patient who has used or needs to use an epinephrine auto-injector.

Advise the patient to use their epinephrine auto-injector if prescribed and available, and to call 911 immediately — anaphylaxis can worsen quickly even after epinephrine.

---

## 11. Gastrointestinal / Abdominal Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Treat as an emergency when a patient describes, now or recently:

- **Sudden, severe abdominal pain**, or a rigid, board-like abdomen.
- **Vomiting blood** or material that looks like coffee grounds.
- **Black, tarry, or bloody stools** with weakness, dizziness, or fainting.
- Severe abdominal pain **with** fever, persistent vomiting, or inability to pass stool or gas.
- Severe pain **with** a pulsating abdominal mass or back pain in an older adult — possible aortic emergency.

---

## 12. Bleeding, Trauma, and Wound Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Treat as an emergency when a patient describes, now or recently:

- **Severe bleeding that will not stop** with pressure.
- A **major injury** — high-speed crash, significant fall, or a blow with loss of consciousness.
- A **head injury** with confusion, repeated vomiting, severe headache, unequal pupils, a seizure, or loss of consciousness.
- A **deep or gaping wound**, a wound with heavy bleeding, or a possible broken bone with deformity.
- **Burns** that are large, deep, or involve the face, hands, genitals, or airway.

---

## 13. Pregnancy-Related Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Treat as an emergency in a pregnant patient who describes, now or recently:

- **Heavy vaginal bleeding** or passing large clots.
- **Severe abdominal or pelvic pain.**
- **Severe headache with vision changes, or sudden severe swelling** — possible dangerous high blood pressure.
- **A seizure** in pregnancy.
- **Decreased or absent fetal movement** in later pregnancy (urgent evaluation; advise prompt contact with the obstetric team or ED).
- **Signs of preterm labor** — regular contractions, fluid leakage, or pelvic pressure before term.

---

## 14. Infection / Sepsis Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Treat as an emergency when a patient describes signs that an infection may be becoming life-threatening (possible sepsis):

- **High fever with confusion, severe weakness, or difficulty staying awake.**
- **Fast breathing or shortness of breath** with a known or suspected infection.
- **A fever with a stiff neck, severe headache, and sensitivity to light** — possible meningitis.
- **A rapidly spreading skin infection**, severe pain out of proportion to the appearance, or skin turning dark — possible severe soft-tissue infection.
- **Low urine output, cold or mottled skin, or feeling like they "might die"** with an infection.

---

## 15. Mental-Health and Toxicology Red Flags (Call 911 / Behavioral Health Crisis Response)

Treat as an emergency when a patient (or proxy) describes, now or recently:

- **Active thoughts of suicide with intent or a plan**, or having taken steps toward self-harm.
- **An overdose or ingestion**, or thoughts of harming others with intent.
- **Severe agitation, threatening behavior, or being out of touch with reality** in a way that endangers the patient or others.
- Any statement that the patient is **in immediate danger** to themselves.

For these, the response combines emergency action (911) with the behavioral-health crisis pathway in Part 5 and BH-600 (Behavioral Health Access Line, 585-230-3911). For suspected poisoning or medication overdose where the patient is awake and breathing, the Poison Information Line (585-230-2700) can advise, but **any unresponsive, not-breathing, seizing, or seriously ill patient is a 911 call first.**

---

## 16. General "Something Is Seriously Wrong" Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

Some emergencies do not fit a single system. Treat as an emergency when a patient describes:

- A symptom they call **"the worst ever"** or that came on **suddenly and severely**.
- **Rapidly worsening** symptoms over minutes to a few hours.
- A strong sense that **"something is very wrong"** or a feeling of impending doom.
- **Collapse, inability to stand, or near-loss of consciousness.**

When in doubt, these guidelines instruct staff to err toward emergency response.

## Part 3 of 7 — Level-of-Care Decision Guidance: ER vs. Urgent Care vs. Telehealth vs. Portal

## 17. The Four Levels of Care

Choosing the right level of care keeps patients safe and avoids both under- and over-treatment. LRMC describes four levels, from highest to lowest acuity:

1. **Emergency Department / 911** — for emergencies and possible time-critical conditions (Part 2). Available 24/7 with full emergency capabilities. LRMC ED information line 585-230-1911; for an emergency, call 911 or go directly.
2. **Urgent care** — for non-life-threatening problems that need prompt, same-day attention but not the ED. Lakeside Urgent Care, 585-230-1700.
3. **Telehealth / virtual care** — for non-urgent and selected urgent issues that do not need a physical exam or testing. LRMC Virtual Care, 585-230-3500.
4. **Portal message / routine appointment** — for non-urgent questions and stable matters, handled under PCT-100.

The overriding rule: **when in doubt about whether something is an emergency, treat it as one.** No cost or convenience consideration justifies steering a possible emergency away from 911 or the ED.

---

## 18. When to Use the Emergency Department or Call 911

Direct the patient to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department for **any red flag in Part 2**, or for any situation the patient or staff believe may be life- or limb-threatening. Call **911 specifically** (rather than driving) when the patient may need treatment on the way, could lose consciousness, has chest pain or stroke signs, has severe breathing trouble, may have a spinal injury, or is too unstable to travel safely. The ED is the right level whenever time-critical treatment, emergency imaging, or resuscitation capability may be needed.

### 18.1 Why 911 Over Self-Transport for Time-Critical Conditions

For heart attack, stroke, severe breathing failure, anaphylaxis, and major trauma, EMS can begin care immediately, alert the hospital, and route the patient to the right facility. These guidelines advise calling 911 rather than driving for these conditions, because treatment can begin sooner and because the patient could deteriorate en route.

---

## 19. When Urgent Care Is Appropriate

Urgent care (Lakeside Urgent Care, 585-230-1700) is appropriate for problems that need prompt, same-day care but are **not** emergencies and have **no Part 2 red flags**, such as:

- Minor injuries — small lacerations that may need stitches, sprains, or suspected minor fractures without deformity.
- Infections — sore throat, ear pain, urinary symptoms without high fever or flank pain, or a localized skin infection without spreading or systemic signs.
- Mild to moderate symptoms — low-grade fever, cough, or vomiting/diarrhea without dehydration or red flags.
- Minor allergic reactions without any breathing or throat involvement.

If a red flag is present or the patient looks or feels very ill, urgent care is **not** the right level — direct to 911/ED.

---

## 20. When Telehealth / Virtual Care Is Appropriate

Telehealth (LRMC Virtual Care, 585-230-3500) is appropriate for issues that can be safely assessed without a physical examination or in-person testing and that have **no red flags**, such as:

- Medication questions, many prescription refills (under RX-500), and follow-up on stable chronic conditions.
- Minor symptoms — mild cold or cough, minor rash, pink eye, or a mild urinary concern in a low-risk patient.
- Behavioral-health support visits when there is no crisis or active safety concern (crisis goes to the pathway in Part 5).
- Review of non-urgent results and general guidance.

Telehealth is **not** for emergencies and not for problems that need hands-on examination, imaging, or labs. When a virtual visit reveals a red flag, the clinician directs the patient to 911/ED or urgent care as appropriate.

---

## 21. When a Portal Message or Routine Appointment Is Appropriate

A portal message or a routine appointment (PCT-100) is appropriate for non-urgent, stable matters with **no red flags** — routine refills, stable chronic-condition questions, non-urgent results questions, scheduling, and general information. Patients are reminded that the portal is **not monitored continuously** and is **not for emergencies**; for an urgent concern after hours they should call the nurse line at 585-230-0911, and for an emergency call 911.

---

## 22. Level-of-Care Decision Sequence

Staff and the automated assistant apply this sequence to a symptom-containing message or call:

1. **Any Part 2 red flag, now or recent?** → Direct to **911/ED**; provide mandatory emergency language (Part 6).
2. **No red flag, but needs prompt same-day attention?** → **Urgent care** (585-230-1700) or a same-day visit; provide safety-netting.
3. **Non-urgent and assessable remotely?** → **Telehealth** (585-230-3500) or nurse guidance.
4. **Non-urgent and stable?** → **Portal/routine appointment** under PCT-100.
5. **Uncertain between two levels?** → Choose the **higher** level and, for clinical uncertainty, route to a nurse. After hours, the nurse line (585-230-0911) helps the patient decide.

Every clinical disposition includes safety-netting: what change would warrant a higher level of care and exactly how to seek it, always including the instruction to call 911 for an emergency.

## Part 4 of 7 — After-Hours Nurse Line (585-230-0911) Protocols

## 23. Purpose and Position of the Nurse Line

The **after-hours nurse line (585-230-0911)** provides live clinical telephone triage when LRMC's ambulatory clinics and portal queues are not actively monitored — nights, weekends, and LRMC-observed holidays. It is operated under PCT-210 and applies the same red-flag and level-of-care content as the rest of these guidelines. The nurse line exists so that a patient with an after-hours concern reaches a **monitored, live** clinical resource instead of waiting for a portal reply. It is **not** a substitute for 911; for an emergency, patients are told to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department.

### 23.1 How Patients Reach the Nurse Line

The after-hours nurse line number (585-230-0911) is provided to patients in the portal after-hours auto-reply, on clinic voicemail greetings, in patient materials, and in the safety-netting of clinical replies. The portal after-hours auto-reply directs patients to 911 for emergencies and to 585-230-0911 for urgent clinical questions that cannot wait until the next business day.

---

## 24. Nurse Line Call-Handling Protocol

### 24.1 Step 1 — Immediate Emergency Screen

At the start of every nurse-line call, the nurse screens for an immediate emergency. If the caller describes any Part 2 red flag, or appears to be in a life-threatening situation, the nurse instructs them to **hang up and call 911, or go to the nearest emergency department now**, stays on the line to confirm the plan when possible, and does not delay this instruction to gather a full history.

### 24.2 Step 2 — Identity and Caller Verification

The nurse verifies the caller's identity or proxy authority consistent with PRIV-400 before discussing protected health information, and confirms whether the call concerns the patient themselves or someone else (a proxy or bystander). For a bystander describing an emergency in another person, the nurse directs them to call 911 for that person immediately.

### 24.3 Step 3 — Focused Assessment

For non-emergency calls, the nurse conducts a focused assessment of the chief concern — onset, severity, associated symptoms, relevant history, and any red flags — applying approved telephone-triage protocols. The assessment determines the appropriate level of care using the decision sequence in Part 3.

### 24.4 Step 4 — Disposition

The nurse assigns one of these dispositions and communicates it clearly:

- **Call 911 / go to the ED now** — for emergencies and red flags.
- **Be seen today/tonight at urgent care or the ED** — for urgent problems needing in-person care.
- **Virtual visit or next-business-day appointment** — for issues that can wait safely.
- **Home care with safety-netting** — for minor, stable symptoms, with explicit warning signs and instructions to call back or escalate if they appear.
- **Pharmacy/medication coordination** — routed under RX-500 (Pharmacy, 585-230-2100) where applicable.

### 24.5 Step 5 — Safety-Netting on Every Call

Every nurse-line call ends with safety-netting: the specific warning signs that should prompt the patient to seek a higher level of care, exactly how to do so, and the standing instruction to call 911 for an emergency. The patient is invited to call back if anything changes or worsens.

### 24.6 Step 6 — Documentation and Handoff

The nurse documents the call in the legal medical record per HIM-300 — the concern, assessment, red-flag screen, disposition, and safety-netting — and, when follow-up is needed the next business day (for example, a provider callback or an appointment), creates an explicit handoff so the day team owns it. Time-critical concerns are not left to chance; the nurse confirms the next owner.

---

## 25. Special Nurse-Line Situations

### 25.1 Behavioral-Health Crisis After Hours

When an after-hours caller expresses suicidal or homicidal ideation, intent, or a crisis, the nurse follows the mental-health-crisis pathway in Part 5 and BH-600: for active intent, plan, overdose, or immediate danger, the response is a 911-level emergency, and the nurse engages the Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911) and crisis resources. For distress without active intent, the nurse provides support, connects the caller to behavioral-health resources, and arranges prompt follow-up. The nurse never minimizes or rushes a self-harm call.

### 25.2 Unreachable High-Risk Caller / Disconnected Calls

If a high-risk caller is disconnected and cannot be reached, the nurse attempts callback on all numbers available and escalates under the failure-to-reach protocol (Part 5), which may include requesting a welfare check under clinician direction and Risk Management guidance (585-230-8100) in a life-threatening situation. All attempts and actions are documented.

### 25.3 Poison and Overdose Calls

For a suspected poisoning or medication overdose in a patient who is **awake and breathing**, the nurse may direct the caller to the Poison Information Line (585-230-2700) for specific guidance while staying alert for deterioration. For any patient who is **unresponsive, not breathing, seizing, or seriously ill**, the nurse directs an immediate **911** call first. Medication-specific questions are coordinated with pharmacy under RX-500.

### 25.4 Pediatric After-Hours Calls

For calls about infants and young children, the nurse applies a lower threshold for urgent and emergency dispositions, recognizing that young children can deteriorate quickly. Red flags such as difficulty breathing, persistent vomiting, poor feeding or dehydration, lethargy or difficulty waking, a febrile young infant, or a non-blanching rash prompt urgent or emergency care.

### 25.5 Capacity, Surge, and Outage

If call volume surges or the nurse line experiences an outage, supervisors ensure emergencies are still prioritized, additional coverage is engaged, and patients are reminded that **911 is always available** for emergencies regardless of nurse-line status. System outages are escalated to the IT Service Desk (585-230-9000) while clinical handling continues by phone.

## Part 5 of 7 — Condition Pathways: Chest Pain, Stroke, Breathing, and Mental-Health Crisis

## 26. How the Pathways Work

The pathways below standardize handling for the highest-risk presentations, where minutes matter and variation is dangerous. Each pathway is self-contained: recognition, immediate action, what to tell the patient, escalation, and documentation. They apply to portal messages (via PCT-100 and the automated assistant), clinic calls, and the after-hours nurse line. In every pathway, the first action for a recognized emergency is to direct the patient to **call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department** — the pathway does not attempt to manage the emergency by message.

## 27. Chest Pain Pathway

### 27.1 Recognize

Activate the chest-pain pathway for **active or recent** chest pain, pressure, tightness, or squeezing — especially lasting more than a few minutes, occurring with exertion, or accompanied by shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, lightheadedness, a sense of doom, or pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck, or back. Atypical presentations are common, particularly in older adults, people with diabetes, and women; do not require a "classic" description before acting.

### 27.2 Immediate Action

Treat as an emergency. Direct the patient to **call 911 now** (not to drive themselves), attempt direct phone contact, and escalate to a clinician immediately per PCT-100. Do not engage in back-and-forth troubleshooting of chest pain by message.

### 27.3 What to Tell the Patient

Tell the patient plainly: "Your symptoms could be a heart problem and need emergency care now. **Please call 911 immediately** — do not drive yourself. If you are not allergic and it is available, you may chew an aspirin while you wait for help, unless a clinician has told you not to take aspirin. Stay on the phone with 911." Include the mandatory emergency language from Part 6.

### 27.4 Escalate and Document

Escalate to the responsible provider/clinician without waiting for the patient's reply, attempt all contact numbers, and, if the patient cannot be reached and the situation is life-threatening, follow the failure-to-reach protocol (Section 31). Document the recognition, the emergency instruction given, contact attempts, escalation, and outcome per HIM-300.

## 28. Stroke Pathway

### 28.1 Recognize

Activate the stroke pathway for **sudden** onset of any BE-FAST sign — **B**alance loss, **E**ye/vision changes, **F**ace drooping, **A**rm/leg weakness or numbness (often one-sided), **S**peech difficulty — or a sudden severe "worst-ever" headache, sudden confusion, or sudden severe weakness/numbness. **Time is brain:** the time of symptom onset is critical, because some stroke treatments are time-limited.

### 28.2 Immediate Action

Treat as an emergency. Direct the patient (or proxy/bystander) to **call 911 immediately**, note and report the **time the symptoms started** (or when the patient was last known well), and not to drive. Escalate to a clinician at once. Do not delay 911 to gather more history.

### 28.3 What to Tell the Patient

Tell the patient or caller plainly: "These can be signs of a stroke, which is an emergency where **every minute matters**. **Call 911 right now** and tell them when the symptoms started. Do not drive — let the ambulance come to you." Include the mandatory emergency language from Part 6.

### 28.4 Escalate and Document

Escalate immediately, attempt direct contact, apply the failure-to-reach protocol if the patient is unreachable and the situation is life-threatening, and document recognition, the time-of-onset captured, the emergency instruction, contact attempts, and outcome per HIM-300.

## 29. Breathing-Difficulty Pathway

### 29.1 Recognize

Activate the breathing pathway for **severe shortness of breath or difficulty breathing at rest**, inability to speak in full sentences, bluish lips or face, choking, a severe asthma or COPD attack not relieved by usual rescue treatment, coughing up blood, or sudden shortness of breath with chest pain or a swollen, painful leg (possible lung clot).

### 29.2 Immediate Action

Treat severe breathing difficulty as an emergency. Direct the patient to **call 911 now**, attempt direct contact, and escalate to a clinician. For a known asthma/COPD patient, advise using their prescribed rescue inhaler as directed while help is on the way, but do not let inhaler use delay a 911 call when breathing is severe or not improving.

### 29.3 What to Tell the Patient

Tell the patient plainly: "Severe trouble breathing is an emergency. **Call 911 now.** If you have a rescue inhaler, use it as prescribed while you wait, but still call 911 if your breathing is severe or not improving. Try to stay calm and sit upright." Include the mandatory emergency language from Part 6.

### 29.4 Escalate and Document

Escalate immediately, attempt contact, apply the failure-to-reach protocol if needed, and document recognition, the emergency instruction, any rescue-treatment guidance, contact attempts, and outcome per HIM-300. Coordinate medication questions with pharmacy under RX-500 (585-230-2100) only after safety is addressed.

## 30. Mental-Health-Crisis Pathway

### 30.1 Recognize

Activate the mental-health-crisis pathway for any message or call indicating **suicidal or homicidal ideation, intent, or plan**, a **report of an overdose or ingestion**, **steps already taken toward self-harm**, severe agitation or being out of touch with reality in a dangerous way, or any statement that the patient is **in immediate danger**. Distress without active intent is handled urgently and supportively (Section 30.5).

### 30.2 Immediate Action for Active Risk

For active suicidal/homicidal intent, a plan, an overdose/ingestion, or immediate danger, treat as a **911-level emergency**: direct the patient to **call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department now**, engage the **Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911)** and crisis resources under BH-600, attempt to keep the patient connected, and attempt direct contact. For overdose/ingestion, treat as the toxicology emergency it is — 911 first for anyone unresponsive, not breathing, seizing, or seriously ill.

### 30.3 What to Tell the Patient

Tell the patient with warmth and directness: "I'm really glad you reached out, and I want to help keep you safe. If you are thinking about harming yourself or are in danger right now, **please call 911, or call or text the national crisis line, or call our Behavioral Health Access Line at 585-230-3911 right now.** You don't have to face this alone." Include the mandatory emergency language from Part 6, adapted with the behavioral-health resources.

### 30.4 Escalate, Failure-to-Reach, and Document

Escalate to behavioral-health and the responsible clinician immediately. If the patient expressed intent and cannot be reached, follow the failure-to-reach protocol (Section 31), which may include a **welfare check** under clinician direction and Risk Management guidance (585-230-8100). Document the risk indicators, actions, resources provided, contact attempts, and outcome per HIM-300 and BH-600. Never minimize, delay, or close a self-harm message without confirming safety or a safe handoff.

### 30.5 Distress Without Active Intent

For significant emotional distress **without** active suicidal/homicidal intent, plan, or immediate danger, treat as **Urgent**: respond supportively, connect the patient to behavioral-health resources (585-230-3911), arrange prompt clinical contact, and provide safety-netting — exactly what to do and whom to call if thoughts of self-harm arise, including 911 and the crisis line. Reassess; if active risk emerges, move to Section 30.2.

## 31. Failure-to-Reach Protocol (All Emergency Pathways)

When an emergency or high-risk message is identified but the patient cannot be reached to confirm they are getting help, staff:

1. **Attempt all contacts** — call every phone number on file, more than once, and send a secure message instructing the patient to call 911 or go to the ED now.
2. **Notify the responsible clinician** and a supervisor, without waiting.
3. **Engage emergency resources** appropriate to the situation; for behavioral-health imminent risk, engage the Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911) and crisis resources.
4. **Consider a welfare check** — in a life-threatening situation where the patient cannot be reached, request a welfare check through appropriate channels under clinician direction and Risk Management guidance (585-230-8100), consistent with PRIV-400.
5. **Document everything** — every attempt, notification, and action, with times and outcomes, per HIM-300.

The protocol does not stop until the patient's safety is confirmed or all reasonable avenues are exhausted and documented.

## Part 6 of 7 — What an Automated or Staff Reply Must Say When Emergency Indicators Appear

## 32. The Mandatory-Reply Rule

When emergency indicators appear in a message — whether flagged by the automated triage-support assistant or recognized by a staff member — any reply that goes to the patient **must** contain the mandatory emergency language defined here, and the message **must** trigger the emergency handling in PCT-100 and the relevant pathway in Part 5. This rule exists because an emergency message must never receive a casual, delayed, or ambiguous reply. The mandatory language is the floor, not the ceiling; staff add pathway-specific guidance (Part 5) as appropriate.

### 32.1 Who This Applies To

This applies to: the automated assistant's **draft** replies; staff replies sent in the portal or by message; and nurse-line verbal guidance (adapted to spoken form). The human-in-the-loop rule from PCT-100 still governs — a qualified human reviews and approves any clinically significant reply before it is sent — but the **content requirements** below are mandatory whenever emergency indicators are present.

---

## 33. Required Elements of an Emergency Reply

Every emergency reply must contain all of the following, in plain language:

1. **A clear statement that the symptoms may be an emergency.** Name the concern in plain terms ("your symptoms could be a heart problem / a stroke / a severe breathing problem / a safety emergency").
2. **A direct instruction to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department now.** This is unambiguous and comes early in the reply.
3. **An instruction not to wait for a reply here.** Tell the patient the portal is not monitored continuously and they should not wait on a message.
4. **A "do not drive" instruction where appropriate** (chest pain, stroke, severe breathing, possible loss of consciousness) — call 911 instead.
5. **Any safe, pathway-specific interim guidance** — for example, chew aspirin for possible cardiac chest pain unless contraindicated; use a prescribed rescue inhaler for asthma; use a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector for anaphylaxis — without letting it delay the 911 call.
6. **Behavioral-health resources where relevant** — call 911, the national crisis line, or the Behavioral Health Access Line at 585-230-3911 for a safety emergency.
7. **A statement that LRMC is also acting** — that staff are trying to reach the patient and have escalated — when true.

### 33.1 What an Emergency Reply Must NOT Do

An emergency reply must **never**:

- Suggest the patient "wait and see," "monitor at home," or "message back if it gets worse" in place of seeking emergency care.
- Provide a routine appointment or refill as the resolution to an emergency message.
- Minimize the symptom, offer a reassuring diagnosis, or talk the patient out of calling 911.
- Be sent by an automated tool without human review when the content is clinically significant.
- Omit the direct 911/ED instruction.

The automated assistant is configured so that, when it detects possible emergency content, its **draft never de-escalates** and always includes the mandatory elements above for a human to review and approve.

## 35. Configuration and Oversight of the Automated Assistant

### 35.1 Fail-Safe Configuration

The automated triage-support assistant is configured so that, when emergency indicators appear, it raises urgency for human review and drafts a reply containing the mandatory elements in Section 33 — it **never** drafts a de-escalating or reassuring reply to possible emergency content, and it **never** sends a clinically significant reply without human approval. If the assistant is uncertain, it errs toward flagging. If it is unavailable, the standard human workflow applies with no loss of safety.

### 35.2 No Auto-Send for Emergencies

No emergency reply is auto-sent. A qualified human reviews and approves every emergency reply before it reaches the patient, consistent with PCT-100. The assistant's role is to surface possible emergencies quickly and propose safe language — not to make or deliver the final clinical decision.

### 35.3 Monitoring

The PCOC monitors the assistant's emergency-recognition performance — how reliably it flags true emergencies (sensitivity) and how often it over-flags — and reviews any case where an emergency was missed or a reply omitted the mandatory language. Findings drive configuration and training updates. The overriding standard is unchanged: **miss no true emergency**, minimize false alarms, and never de-escalate by tool.

## Part 7 of 7 — Common Patient Questions (FAQ), Quality Monitoring, and Appendices

## 36. Common Patient Questions

These are phrased the way patients ask them, with policy-grounded answers staff can adapt. Each stands alone for retrieval.

### 36.1 "Should I go to the ER or urgent care?"

Go to the **emergency department, or call 911**, for anything that could be life-threatening — chest pain, signs of a stroke (sudden face drooping, arm weakness, or trouble speaking), severe trouble breathing, severe bleeding, a severe allergic reaction, or thoughts of harming yourself. Use **urgent care** (Lakeside Urgent Care, 585-230-1700) for problems that need same-day care but aren't emergencies, like minor injuries, infections, or moderate symptoms. If you're not sure, treat it as an emergency, or call our after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911 for help deciding.

### 36.2 "When should I call 911 instead of driving?"

Call **911** — don't drive — for possible heart attack or stroke, severe trouble breathing, a severe allergic reaction, major injuries, or any time you might pass out. Ambulance crews can start care right away and get you to the right place fast. Driving yourself with these conditions is dangerous because you could get worse on the way.

### 36.3 "Can I just message my doctor about chest pain?"

No — please don't wait on a message for chest pain. Portal messages aren't monitored all the time, and chest pain can be an emergency. **Call 911 right away**, especially if the pain comes with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or spreads to your arm or jaw. You can tell your care team afterward, but get emergency help first.

### 36.4 "What is the after-hours nurse line for?"

The after-hours nurse line (**585-230-0911**) lets you talk to a nurse at night, on weekends, and on holidays when our clinics are closed. The nurse can help you figure out whether you need the ER, urgent care, a visit, or home care, and what to watch for. It's **not** for emergencies — for those, call 911 — but it's a great resource when you're not sure what to do and it can't wait until morning.

### 36.5 "I think I'm having an allergic reaction — what do I do?"

If you have **swelling of your lips, tongue, or throat, trouble breathing, or widespread hives with faintness**, treat it as an emergency: if you have an epinephrine auto-injector, use it as prescribed and then **call 911**. If you don't have one, call 911 right away. Severe allergic reactions can get worse fast, so you need emergency care even if the epinephrine helps at first.

### 36.6 "When is telehealth okay to use?"

Telehealth (LRMC Virtual Care, **585-230-3500**) works well for things that don't need a physical exam — medication questions, many refills, follow-ups on stable conditions, minor symptoms like a cold or pink eye, and behavioral-health support visits when there's no crisis. It's **not** for emergencies or anything that needs hands-on exam or testing. If a virtual visit turns up something serious, the clinician will direct you to the right level of care.

### 36.7 "I'm having thoughts of hurting myself. What should I do?"

Please reach out for help right now — you don't have to handle this alone. If you're in danger or thinking about harming yourself, **call 911, call or text the national crisis line, or call our Behavioral Health Access Line at 585-230-3911 immediately.** These are available any time. If you've already taken something or hurt yourself, call 911 now.

### 36.8 "My child has a fever — is that an emergency?"

Most fevers in children aren't emergencies, but call 911 or go to the ER if your child has **trouble breathing, won't wake up or is very sleepy, has a stiff neck with a severe headache, has a rash that doesn't fade when pressed, has a seizure, or seems very ill or dehydrated**. A very young infant with a fever should be evaluated promptly. If you're unsure, call our after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911.

### 36.9 "I think I took too much of my medication — what now?"

If the person is **unresponsive, not breathing, seizing, or seriously ill, call 911 immediately.** If they are awake and breathing, you can call the Poison Information Line at 585-230-2700 for guidance, and contact us as well. Don't wait to see what happens with serious symptoms — call 911.

### 36.10 "Why did you tell me to call 911 when I just sent a message?"

Because what you described could be an emergency, and the safest thing we can do is make sure you get immediate help. Portal messages aren't watched around the clock, so we never want you to wait on a message when minutes could matter. We'd much rather send you to the ER and have it turn out to be minor than risk a delay with something serious.

## 37. Quality Monitoring and Oversight

### 37.1 Emergency-Recognition Review

A sample of messages and calls containing symptoms is reviewed regularly to confirm that red flags were recognized, the correct level of care was advised, the mandatory emergency language was present when indicated, pathways were followed, and documentation met HIM-300. Any missed or delayed emergency recognition is reviewed as a safety event.

### 37.2 Metrics

The PCOC, with ED and Behavioral Health leadership, reviews metrics including time-to-identification of emergency messages, adherence to the mandatory-reply rule, nurse-line disposition accuracy and call timeliness, automated-assistant sensitivity and over-flag rate, and outcomes of failure-to-reach and welfare-check cases. Metrics are used to improve recognition; none is permitted to incentivize under-triage.

### 37.3 Safety-Event Reporting

Missed emergencies, omitted emergency language, delayed escalations, and near-misses are reported through the non-punitive safety-event system and reviewed by the PCOC, with serious events escalated to Compliance / Risk Management (585-230-8100). The organization uses these reviews to strengthen recognition and pathways.

### 37.4 Drills and Competency

Staff and the nurse-line team complete periodic competency checks and scenario drills on the chest-pain, stroke, breathing, and mental-health-crisis pathways, and on the failure-to-reach protocol. Training is updated when pathways, guidelines, or the automated assistant change.

---

## 38. Training Requirements

No staff member handles messages or calls under these guidelines until they complete training covering: the red-flag lists (Part 2); the level-of-care decision sequence (Part 3); the nurse-line protocol (Part 4); the four condition pathways and the failure-to-reach protocol (Part 5); and the mandatory emergency-reply rule and templates (Part 6). Nurse-line staff complete additional telephone-triage competency under PCT-210. Behavioral-health-crisis handling is trained under BH-600. Refresher training and drills are completed periodically and after any significant change.

- **Heart:** chest pain/pressure, especially with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or pain to arm/jaw/back; fainting.

- **Bleeding/Trauma:** uncontrolled bleeding, major injury, head injury with confusion/vomiting.
- **Abdomen:** sudden severe or rigid abdomen, vomiting blood, black/bloody stools with weakness.
- **Pregnancy:** heavy bleeding, severe pain, severe headache with vision changes, seizure, no fetal movement, preterm labor.
- **Infection/Sepsis:** high fever with confusion, fast breathing, stiff neck with light sensitivity, rapidly spreading skin infection.
