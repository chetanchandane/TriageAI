## Part 1 of 8 — Purpose, Scope, Governance, Definitions, and Workflow Overview

## 1. About This Manual

### 1.1 Title and Identification

This is the **Patient Communication and Triage Policy Manual** of Lakeside Regional Medical Center (LRMC). It is policy document **PCT-100** within the LRMC Administrative Policy Library. It governs how LRMC staff receive, classify, prioritize, respond to, escalate, and document non-urgent and urgent patient communications that arrive through the **MyLRMC** patient portal and associated electronic messaging channels.

LRMC is a 400-bed community teaching hospital and affiliated outpatient network located at 1200 Lakeshore Parkway, Rochester, NY 14600. The hospital main line is 585-230-0000. The MyLRMC Patient Portal Help Desk is 585-230-3000. Throughout this manual, "the organization," "LRMC," and "the Medical Center" refer to Lakeside Regional Medical Center and its affiliated clinics unless a specific department is named.

### 1.2 Why This Manual Exists

Patient-portal messaging is now one of the most common ways patients reach Lakeside Regional Medical Center. Patients use MyLRMC to ask clinical questions, request prescription refills, report symptoms, follow up on test results, schedule or change appointments, and resolve administrative matters. A consistent, safe, and timely process for handling these messages protects patients and supports the clinical teams who answer them.

This manual exists to accomplish four goals:

1. **Patient safety first.** Some portal messages describe genuine emergencies. The triage process is designed to detect time-sensitive or life-threatening situations quickly and route the patient to emergency care, rather than leaving an urgent message in a routine queue.
2. **Consistency.** Two patients sending similar messages should receive comparable handling regardless of which staff member opens the message or which clinic the message reaches.
3. **Appropriate scope.** Staff who respond to messages must stay within the limits of their training, licensure, and the standing protocols approved by LRMC clinical leadership. This manual defines those limits.
4. **A defensible record.** Every clinically significant communication must be documented in the legal medical record so that future care is informed and the organization can demonstrate appropriate, timely follow-up.

### 1.3 How to Use This Manual

Each numbered section is written to stand on its own. If you are reading a single section — for example, the definition of an "Urgent" message or the documentation requirements for a refill request — you should be able to act correctly from that section alone, without first reading the whole manual. Cross-references are provided where helpful, but the essential rule of each section is always restated within that section.

When a section gives a timeframe (for example, "Routine messages receive a response within 2 business days"), that timeframe is a **policy standard**, not a suggestion. Departures from a standard must be documented with the reason, as described in Part 6.

### 1.4 Relationship to Other LRMC Policies

This manual works alongside, and does not replace, the following related LRMC policies. When a conflict appears between this manual and one of the policies below, the more protective patient-safety standard governs, and the conflict should be reported to the Patient Communication Oversight Committee (see Section 4).

- **PCT-210 — Telephone Triage and Nurse Advice Line Policy.** Governs spoken telephone triage, including the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911. Portal triage in this manual mirrors the clinical standards of PCT-210 but adapts them to asynchronous written messages.
- **HIM-300 — Documentation and the Legal Medical Record.** Defines what becomes part of the permanent record and how late entries and corrections are handled.
- **PRIV-400 — Patient Privacy and Confidential Communications.** Governs identity verification, proxy and caregiver access, and protection of sensitive information in messages.
- **RX-500 — Outpatient Prescription and Refill Management.** Governs medication refill authorization, controlled-substance handling, and pharmacy coordination (LRMC Outpatient Pharmacy, 585-230-2100).
- **BH-600 — Behavioral Health Access and Suicide-Risk Response.** Governs handling of messages indicating self-harm, suicidal ideation, or behavioral-health crisis (Behavioral Health Access Line, 585-230-3911).
- **PRel-700 — Patient Relations and Complaint Resolution.** Governs service complaints and grievances (Patient Relations, 585-230-5500).

### 1.5 A Note on Medical Advice

This manual is an operational policy document. Nothing in it constitutes individualized medical advice. Clinical guidance in this manual is generic and is applied to specific patients only through the judgment of qualified clinical staff. All clinically significant patient-facing messages are reviewed and approved by qualified staff before release.

## 2. Scope and Applicability

### 2.1 What This Manual Covers

This manual applies to all **asynchronous, text-based patient communications** received by Lakeside Regional Medical Center, including:

- Messages sent through the **MyLRMC** patient portal (clinical questions, symptom reports, refill requests, results inquiries, administrative requests).
- Secure replies a patient sends in response to a portal message initiated by an LRMC staff member.
- Messages submitted through the MyLRMC mobile application, which feed the same queues as the web portal.
- Proxy messages sent by an authorized caregiver, parent, or guardian on behalf of a patient, when proxy access has been verified under PRIV-400.

The clinical-classification principles in this manual (the four urgency categories in Part 2) also serve as the reference standard for staff handling patient email and patient-initiated text-message programs, where those channels are approved and active.

### 2.2 What This Manual Does Not Cover

This manual does **not** govern:

- **Live, synchronous telephone calls**, which are governed by PCT-210 (Telephone Triage). A phone caller describing an emergency should be handled under telephone-triage standards, not asked to send a portal message.
- **In-person clinical encounters** and walk-in presentations to any LRMC clinic or the Emergency Department.
- **Emergency Medical Services (EMS) and 911 dispatch.** LRMC does not operate the regional 911 system. Staff direct patients to call 911 for emergencies; staff do not act as a substitute for 911.
- **Internal clinician-to-clinician messaging** within the electronic health record that does not involve patient-originated content.
- **Billing-only disputes and financial-assistance applications**, which are routed to Patient Financial Services (585-230-7000) and governed by the LRMC Financial Services policy library, although a message that mixes a clinical concern with a billing question is triaged for its clinical content first.

### 2.3 Who Must Follow This Manual

This manual applies to every LRMC workforce member who reads, classifies, answers, routes, or supervises patient portal communications, including:

- Registered nurses (RNs) and licensed practical nurses (LPNs) performing message triage.
- Physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, and other licensed independent practitioners who answer clinical messages or co-sign delegated responses.
- Medical assistants (MAs) and clinical support staff who handle administrative and protocol-eligible messages.
- Patient Access, scheduling, and front-desk staff who handle non-clinical requests.
- Pharmacists and pharmacy technicians coordinating refills through the portal (585-230-2100).
- Supervisors, clinic managers, and members of the Patient Communication Oversight Committee.
- Temporary, per-diem, contract, and student personnel who have been granted portal access. Access is not granted until the individual has completed the training described in Part 8.

### 2.4 Patient Eligibility and Channel Limits

The MyLRMC portal is offered to established LRMC patients age 18 and older, and to authorized proxies of pediatric and dependent-adult patients under PRIV-400. The portal is a tool for **non-emergency** communication. This limitation is stated to every patient at enrollment, is displayed on the message-composition screen, and is repeated in automated acknowledgments:

> **MyLRMC is not for emergencies.** If you are having a medical emergency, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. Do not use portal messages to report an emergency, because messages are not monitored continuously.

Despite this notice, patients sometimes report emergencies by message. The triage workflow in this manual is built on the assumption that emergency content **will** arrive through the portal and must be detected and acted upon, regardless of the channel's intended use.

## 3. Guiding Principles

The following principles govern every decision in this manual. When a situation is not specifically addressed by a later section, staff apply these principles.

### 3.1 Safety Before Convenience

A patient's safety always outranks workflow efficiency, queue metrics, and response-time targets. When a message could describe a time-sensitive or dangerous condition, staff treat it as the more urgent possibility until a qualified clinician determines otherwise. It is always acceptable to escalate a message; it is never acceptable to downgrade a potentially dangerous message to clear a queue.

### 3.2 Read for the Worst Plausible Meaning

Patients describe symptoms in everyday language, often understating severity. Staff read each message for the **worst reasonable interpretation** consistent with the words used, and triage to that interpretation. A message that says "a little chest pressure since this morning" is evaluated as possible cardiac chest pain, not dismissed as indigestion, until a clinician has assessed it.

### 3.3 Right Person, Right Scope

Every message is answered by someone qualified to answer it. Clinical questions requiring assessment, judgment, or medical advice are handled by licensed clinical staff or routed to the responsible provider. Administrative and protocol-eligible messages may be handled by trained support staff. No staff member answers outside the scope defined in Part 4.

### 3.4 Close the Loop

A message is not "done" when a reply is sent. It is done when the patient's need has been addressed or safely handed off, the action taken is documented, and any required follow-up (a callback, a test order, an appointment, an escalation) has been confirmed or scheduled. Safety-netting — telling the patient what to watch for and what to do if things change — is part of closing the loop.

### 3.5 Document as You Go

If a clinically significant communication is not documented in the legal medical record, it is treated, for quality and legal purposes, as if it did not happen. Documentation is contemporaneous: staff record the message, the assessment, the advice given, and the disposition at the time of handling, not retrospectively. Part 6 defines the standard.

### 3.6 Plain Language and Respect

Replies are written in plain, courteous English at a reading level accessible to a general audience. Staff avoid unexplained jargon, blame, and judgmental phrasing. Interpreter Services (585-230-6400) and translated materials are offered to patients with limited English proficiency, and the portal's accessibility features are honored for patients with disabilities.

### 3.7 Privacy by Default

Staff verify they are communicating with the patient or an authorized proxy before releasing protected health information, follow PRIV-400 for sensitive categories, and include only the minimum necessary information in each reply. Sensitive results and diagnoses are released according to the results-communication rules summarized in Part 4 and detailed in HIM-300.

## 4. Governance, Roles, and Accountability

### 4.1 Policy Ownership and Oversight

This manual is owned by the **Patient Communication Oversight Committee (PCOC)** of Lakeside Regional Medical Center. The PCOC is co-chaired by the Chief Nursing Officer's designee and the Ambulatory Medical Director, and includes representatives from nursing, primary and specialty care, pharmacy, health information management, patient relations, behavioral health, compliance/risk management, and patient portal administration. The PCOC reviews this manual at least **annually**, and sooner when a safety event, regulatory change, or workflow change requires it. The committee can be reached through Patient Relations at 585-230-5500.

### 4.2 Roles and Responsibilities

**Triage Nurse (RN).** Performs clinical classification of messages with symptom or clinical content, applies the urgency categories in Part 2, provides nurse advice within approved protocols, escalates per Part 5, and documents per Part 6. The Triage Nurse Coordination Desk is reachable internally at 585-230-1800.

**Responsible Provider.** The physician, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant accountable for the patient's care. Answers messages requiring medical judgment, authorizes orders and prescriptions, and co-signs delegated clinical responses where required by Part 4.

**Clinical Support Staff (MA/LPN).** Handles administrative and protocol-eligible messages, gathers information for the triage nurse or provider, and routes messages that exceed their scope. Does not provide medical advice.

**Patient Access / Scheduling Staff.** Handles appointment, registration, and general administrative messages through Central Scheduling (585-230-2600). Routes any message containing clinical content to clinical staff.

**Pharmacy Staff.** Coordinates refill requests and medication questions in conjunction with the responsible provider, through the LRMC Outpatient Pharmacy (585-230-2100), under RX-500.

**Clinic Manager / Supervisor.** Monitors queue volumes and response-time performance, manages coverage and out-of-office reassignment, handles overflow, and supports staff in difficult cases.

**Portal Administrator.** Manages MyLRMC accounts, proxy access, message-routing configuration, and the help desk (585-230-3000). Does not perform clinical triage.

**Compliance / Risk Management.** Advises on privacy, consent, and risk questions and receives reports of safety events. Reachable at 585-230-8100.

### 4.3 Accountability for Every Message

Each message in the system has, at all times, an identifiable **owner** — the staff member or role currently responsible for the next action. Ownership transfers explicitly: when a message is routed or escalated, the sending staff member confirms the receiving owner has accepted it, and the transfer is documented. A message is never "in the queue but owned by no one." Supervisors audit for ownerless or aging messages daily, as described in Part 8.

### 4.4 Non-Punitive Reporting

LRMC maintains a non-punitive culture for reporting near-misses and errors in message handling. Staff who identify a mis-triaged message, a missed escalation, or a delayed response are expected to report it through the safety-event system so the organization can learn and improve. Honest reporting is protected; concealment is not.

## 5. Definitions

The following terms are used throughout this manual. Each definition is written to stand alone so that a reader encountering the term in any Part can resolve it here.

**Asynchronous communication.** A written message exchange in which the sender and recipient are not interacting in real time. Portal messages are asynchronous: a patient may send a message at any hour, and staff respond during operational hours. Asynchronous channels are unsuitable for emergencies because no one is guaranteed to be reading them at the moment a message arrives.

**Triage.** The process of reviewing an incoming patient message, determining its clinical urgency and subject, and routing it to the right owner for action within the right timeframe. In this manual, triage specifically means **portal-message triage**, the classification of written messages into the four urgency categories defined in Part 2.

**Urgency category.** One of four classifications — **Emergency, Urgent, Routine, or Informational** — assigned to every triaged message. The category determines the required response time and the required handling, as defined in Part 2 and Part 3.

**Emergency (category).** A message describing a known or possible life-threatening or limb-threatening condition, or a condition requiring immediate in-person evaluation. Emergency-category messages trigger immediate action to direct the patient to 911 or emergency care and to attempt direct patient contact. Detailed criteria and examples are in Part 2.

**Urgent (category).** A message describing a condition that is not immediately life-threatening but requires prompt clinical attention, typically the same day or within a defined number of hours, to prevent deterioration or to relieve significant symptoms. Detailed criteria and examples are in Part 2.

**Routine (category).** A message describing a non-urgent clinical or care-coordination matter that can be safely handled within standard business timeframes — for example, a stable chronic-condition question, a routine refill, or a non-urgent results question. Detailed criteria and examples are in Part 2.

**Informational (category).** A message that does not require clinical assessment — for example, an administrative request, a scheduling change, a billing question, or an acknowledgment that needs no clinical reply. Detailed criteria and examples are in Part 2.

**Safety-netting.** Explicit guidance given to a patient about what symptoms or changes should prompt them to seek more urgent care, and exactly how to do so (for example, "If the pain spreads to your arm or jaw, or you become short of breath, call 911 immediately"). Safety-netting is required on clinical replies as specified in Part 4 and Part 7.

**Escalation.** The act of moving a message to a higher level of clinical authority or urgency — for example, from support staff to a nurse, from a nurse to a provider, or from routine handling to immediate emergency action. Escalation paths are defined in Part 5.

**Standing protocol / approved protocol.** A written, clinically approved procedure that authorizes specifically trained staff to take defined actions (such as advising on a common symptom or processing a routine refill) without seeking individual provider authorization each time. Protocols are approved by the PCOC and the relevant medical director.

**Scope of practice.** The set of activities a staff member is legally and organizationally authorized to perform, based on licensure, certification, training, and LRMC protocols. Part 4 defines what each role may and may not advise by message.

**Legal medical record.** The official, permanent record of a patient's care maintained by LRMC, governed by HIM-300. Clinically significant portal communications and the actions taken in response become part of this record. Health Information Management is reachable at 585-230-4200.

**Proxy.** An individual authorized under PRIV-400 to access a patient's portal and communicate on the patient's behalf — for example, a parent of a minor or the caregiver of a dependent adult. Proxy status must be verified before protected health information is shared.

**Responsible provider.** The clinician accountable for a patient's care for the matter at hand. When a message requires medical judgment beyond approved nurse protocols, the responsible provider is the owner of the clinical decision.

**Business day / business hours.** LRMC ambulatory business hours are Monday through Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time, excluding LRMC-observed holidays. Response-time standards expressed in "business days" are measured against these hours. Coverage outside these hours is described in Part 3.

**After-hours.** Any time outside ambulatory business hours, including nights, weekends, and LRMC-observed holidays. After-hours clinical needs are served by the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911 and, for emergencies, by 911 and the LRMC Emergency Department.

## 6. The Portal-Triage Workflow Overview

This section gives the high-level flow of a patient message through Lakeside Regional Medical Center, from arrival to closure. Each stage is summarized here and detailed in later Parts. The flow is written so that a reader who needs only the "big picture" can understand the whole process from this section alone.

### 6.1 The Six Stages at a Glance

Every patient portal message moves through six stages:

1. **Arrival and acknowledgment.** The message enters MyLRMC and is automatically acknowledged to the patient with the expected handling timeframe and the standing emergency notice.
2. **Initial review and identity check.** A staff member opens the message, confirms it is from the patient or a verified proxy, and reads it for clinical content.
3. **Urgency classification (triage).** The message is assigned one of four urgency categories — Emergency, Urgent, Routine, or Informational — using the criteria in Part 2.
4. **Routing and ownership.** The message is routed to the correct owner (provider, triage nurse, support staff, pharmacy, scheduling, etc.), and ownership is explicitly accepted.
5. **Response or escalation.** The owner responds within the required timeframe, escalates if needed, and provides safety-netting on clinical replies.
6. **Documentation and closure.** The communication, assessment, advice, and disposition are documented in the legal medical record, and the message is closed only when the patient's need is addressed or safely handed off.

### 6.2 Stage 1 — Arrival and Acknowledgment

When a patient submits a message through MyLRMC, the system immediately returns an automated acknowledgment that (a) confirms receipt, (b) states the expected response timeframe for non-urgent messages, and (c) repeats the emergency notice directing the patient to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department for emergencies. The acknowledgment also reminds the patient that portal messages are **not monitored continuously**, which is why emergencies must not be reported by message. Acknowledgment is automatic and does not constitute triage; a human has not yet read the message at this stage.

### 6.3 Stage 2 — Initial Review and Identity Check

A staff member with portal access opens the message during operational hours and performs two quick checks before triaging:

1. **Identity / proxy check.** Confirm the message is from the patient or a proxy authorized under PRIV-400. If the sender's authority is unclear, the staff member follows the identity-verification steps in PRIV-400 before releasing any protected health information.
2. **Content scan.** Read the entire message — not only the subject line — for any clinical content, including symptoms, medication issues, or distress. Patients frequently bury an urgent symptom inside an administrative request (for example, a scheduling message that mentions new chest pain). The whole message is read before a category is assigned.

### 6.4 Stage 3 — Urgency Classification (Triage)

The staff member assigns one of four urgency categories using the criteria in Part 2. The guiding rule from Section 3 applies: read for the worst plausible meaning, and when in doubt, classify upward. A message may contain more than one issue; when issues differ in urgency, the **most urgent issue sets the category** for handling, and the remaining issues are addressed within their own timeframes. Only clinical staff assign Emergency or Urgent categories; support staff who encounter potential Emergency or Urgent content immediately escalate to a nurse rather than classifying it themselves (see Part 4 and Part 5).

### 6.5 Stage 4 — Routing and Ownership

Based on category and subject, the message is routed to the correct owner:

- **Clinical questions and symptoms** route to the triage nurse or responsible provider.
- **Refill requests** route to pharmacy coordination and the responsible provider under RX-500 (Pharmacy, 585-230-2100).
- **Results inquiries** route per the results-communication rules in Part 4.
- **Scheduling and registration** route to Central Scheduling (585-230-2600).
- **Billing-only** items route to Patient Financial Services (585-230-7000).
- **Behavioral-health crisis content** routes immediately under BH-600 to the Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911) and, for imminent risk, to emergency action.

Ownership is explicit: the receiving owner accepts the message, and the handoff is documented. No message is left without an owner.

### 6.6 Stage 5 — Response or Escalation

The owner either responds within the required timeframe (Part 3) or escalates (Part 5). Clinical replies are written within the responder's scope (Part 4), use plain language, and include safety-netting where a symptom or condition is involved. If at any point the message reveals an Emergency or Urgent situation, the responder stops routine handling and follows the escalation path immediately, attempting direct patient contact rather than relying on a return message the patient may not read promptly.

### 6.7 Stage 6 — Documentation and Closure

The owner documents the communication and the action taken in the legal medical record per Part 6, including the urgency category assigned, the advice or information given, any orders or escalations, and the safety-netting provided. The message is closed only when the patient's need has been addressed or safely handed off and any required follow-up is confirmed or scheduled. A reply alone does not close a message that requires follow-up.

### 6.8 Where the Automated Triage Assistant Fits

Lakeside Regional Medical Center uses an automated triage-support assistant that screens incoming portal messages for possible emergency content and proposes an urgency category and a policy-grounded draft reply for staff review. This tool **supports** the workflow above; it does not replace human judgment. Two rules govern its use, and both are restated wherever the tool is discussed in this manual:

1. **Human-in-the-loop is mandatory for non-routine replies.** A licensed staff member reviews, edits as needed, and approves any clinical reply before it is sent. The assistant's draft is a starting point, never an automatically delivered clinical answer for Emergency, Urgent, or otherwise clinically significant messages.
2. **The assistant fails safe, and so do staff.** If the tool is unavailable or uncertain, messages are handled by the standard human workflow with no loss of safety. A tool that flags possible emergency content raises urgency for human review; a tool that does not flag content never lowers the urgency a human would otherwise assign. Staff remain responsible for the final classification and response.

## Part 2 of 8 — Urgency Categories: Criteria, Examples, and Decision Rules

## 7. The Four Urgency Categories

Every triaged portal message at Lakeside Regional Medical Center is assigned exactly one of four urgency categories — **Emergency, Urgent, Routine, or Informational**. The category sets the required response time (Part 3), the required handling, and who must own the message. This Part defines each category with concrete criteria and examples, and gives the decision rules for choosing between them. The categories are designed for safe asynchronous triage: because a written message may not be read for hours, the system errs toward the more urgent category whenever the wording leaves real doubt.

### 7.1 The Overriding Rule: Classify Upward When in Doubt

When a message could reasonably fit two categories, staff assign the **more urgent** of the two and let a higher-level clinician confirm or downgrade. This rule reflects the guiding principle of reading for the worst plausible meaning. It is always acceptable to escalate; it is never acceptable to downgrade a potentially dangerous message to meet a metric. A clinician may safely de-escalate after assessment; support staff and the automated assistant never de-escalate.

### 7.2 The Most-Urgent-Issue Rule for Mixed Messages

Many messages contain more than one request — for example, a refill plus a new symptom, or a scheduling question plus a report of chest pain. The **most urgent issue sets the category** and the handling timeline for the whole message. The less urgent issues are still addressed, each within its own timeframe, but they never delay action on the most urgent issue. Staff explicitly note each issue so none is dropped.

## 8. Emergency Category

### 8.1 Definition

An **Emergency** message describes a known or possible life-threatening or limb-threatening condition, or a condition that requires immediate in-person evaluation. Emergency messages demand immediate action: directing the patient to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department, and attempting direct contact with the patient rather than relying on a return message. Because the portal is not monitored continuously, an Emergency-category message also triggers the escalation steps in Part 5 to confirm the patient is getting care.

### 8.2 Concrete Criteria

Treat a message as Emergency when it describes, or could reasonably describe, any of the following:

- **Chest pain or pressure**, especially with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck, or back — possible heart attack.
- **Signs of stroke** — sudden face drooping, arm weakness, difficulty speaking, sudden confusion, sudden severe headache, sudden vision loss, or sudden numbness on one side.
- **Difficulty breathing** or severe shortness of breath at rest.
- **Severe bleeding** that will not stop, or vomiting or coughing up blood.
- **Signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)** — swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat; trouble breathing or swallowing; widespread hives with faintness.
- **Statements of intent to harm self or others**, active suicidal or homicidal ideation, or a report of an overdose or ingestion — also routed under BH-600 (Behavioral Health Access Line, 585-230-3911).
- **Sudden severe abdominal pain**, a rigid abdomen, or severe pain with vomiting.
- **Loss of consciousness, a seizure, or unresponsiveness.**
- **Pregnancy emergencies** — heavy vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, or signs of severe high blood pressure such as severe headache with vision changes in a pregnant patient.
- **Sudden severe symptoms** the patient describes as "the worst ever," rapidly worsening, or accompanied by a sense of doom.

### 8.3 Examples of Emergency Messages

- "I've had crushing chest pain for an hour and now my left arm is numb."
- "My husband's face is drooping and he can't speak clearly — it started 20 minutes ago."
- "I took all my pills. I don't want to be here anymore."
- "I'm 30 weeks pregnant and I'm bleeding heavily and cramping."
- "I can't catch my breath even sitting still and my lips feel tingly after a bee sting."

### 8.4 Required Handling

For an Emergency-category message, staff: (1) immediately reply with the standing emergency instruction to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department now; (2) attempt to reach the patient directly by phone; (3) escalate to a clinician and, for behavioral-health emergencies, to the Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911) per Part 5; and (4) document the message, the action taken, and the outcome. Staff do not wait for the patient to reply before escalating. The detailed escalation and safety-net steps are in Part 5.

## 9. Urgent Category

### 9.1 Definition

An **Urgent** message describes a condition that is not immediately life-threatening but requires prompt clinical attention — typically the same day, or within a defined number of hours — to prevent deterioration, relieve significant symptoms, or evaluate a concerning but not emergent finding. Urgent messages are owned by clinical staff and are escalated to the responsible provider or triage nurse promptly. When an Urgent message cannot be resolved by message within its timeframe, the patient is contacted directly and offered a same-day visit, an urgent-care option, or other prompt evaluation.

### 9.2 Concrete Criteria

Treat a message as Urgent when it describes, or could reasonably describe, any of the following:

- **Fever with concerning features** — for example, a high fever with stiff neck, a fever in an immunocompromised patient, or a persistent high fever.
- **Moderate to severe pain** that is new or worsening but without emergency features.
- **A possible infection needing prompt evaluation** — for example, increasing redness, swelling, warmth, or drainage around a wound; a red, hot, swollen joint; or a urinary infection with fever and flank pain.
- **Dehydration risk** — persistent vomiting or diarrhea, especially in older adults or those unable to keep fluids down.
- **New or worsening shortness of breath** that is not severe or at rest, or a significant asthma or COPD flare responding partially to usual treatment.
- **Concerning medication problems** — a new significant side effect, a missed essential medication with symptoms, or a possible adverse reaction that is not anaphylaxis.
- **A new neurologic or visual symptom** that is not clearly a stroke but warrants prompt evaluation.
- **Mental-health distress** that is significant but without active suicidal or homicidal intent — handled under BH-600 with prompt clinical contact.
- **A clinically concerning test result** the patient is asking about that requires timely provider follow-up.

### 9.3 Examples of Urgent Messages

- "My surgical incision is getting red and warm and there's yellow drainage today."
- "I've been throwing up since last night and can't keep any water down."
- "My blood sugar has been over 350 all day and I feel awful."
- "I'm feeling really down and hopeless lately, but I'm not going to hurt myself."
- "My asthma inhaler isn't helping as much and I'm wheezing more than usual."

### 9.4 Required Handling

For an Urgent-category message, clinical staff respond and, where needed, contact the patient directly within the Urgent timeframe defined in Part 3, arrange same-day or prompt evaluation as appropriate, and provide clear safety-netting about what would make the situation an emergency and to call 911 if so. Behavioral-health distress is routed under BH-600. If reassessment suggests the situation is worse than first described, the message is reclassified Emergency and handled accordingly.

## 10. Routine Category

### 10.1 Definition

A **Routine** message describes a non-urgent clinical or care-coordination matter that can be safely handled within standard business timeframes. Routine messages include stable chronic-condition questions, routine medication refills, non-urgent results questions, and follow-up questions after a recent visit. Routine messages are still read promptly for any hidden urgent content; only after confirming none is present are they handled on the routine timeline.

### 10.2 Concrete Criteria

Treat a message as Routine when it describes a clinical or care matter that is stable and non-time-critical, such as:

- A **routine refill** of a stable medication with no new symptoms (processed under RX-500; Pharmacy 585-230-2100).
- A **stable chronic-condition question** — for example, a question about a long-standing, well-controlled condition.
- A **non-urgent results question** about a result that is not clinically concerning.
- A **general medication question** — for example, timing or how to take a medication, without a concerning side effect.
- A **follow-up question** after a recent visit that does not describe worsening.
- A **request for advice on a minor, stable symptom** — for example, a mild cold without warning signs.

### 10.3 Examples of Routine Messages

- "Can I get a refill of my blood-pressure medication? Everything's been stable."
- "My cholesterol result is in the portal — is that number okay?"
- "Should I take my thyroid pill in the morning or at night?"
- "I had a mild cold for two days, no fever — anything I should do?"

### 10.4 Required Handling

Routine messages are handled within the Routine timeframe in Part 3, by the appropriate owner and within scope (Part 4). Refills follow RX-500. Even routine clinical replies include brief safety-netting when a symptom is involved — telling the patient what change would warrant prompt or emergency care. If a "routine"-looking message in fact contains an urgent symptom on full reading, it is reclassified upward immediately.

---

## 11. Informational Category

### 11.1 Definition

An **Informational** message does not require clinical assessment. It is an administrative, logistical, or acknowledgment message — for example, a scheduling change, a records request, a billing question, an insurance update, or a simple thank-you that needs no clinical reply. Informational messages are routed to the appropriate non-clinical owner. The critical safety rule is that support staff still read the whole message for clinical content before treating it as Informational, because patients sometimes attach a symptom to an administrative request.

### 11.2 Concrete Criteria

Treat a message as Informational when it involves only non-clinical matters, such as:

- **Scheduling** — booking, changing, or canceling an appointment (Central Scheduling, 585-230-2600).
- **Registration and demographics** — updating address, phone, or insurance.
- **Billing and financial** — a billing question or financial-assistance inquiry (Patient Financial Services, 585-230-7000).
- **Records** — requesting a copy of records or a form (Health Information Management, 585-230-4200).
- **Portal/technical** — a login or technical issue (MyLRMC Help Desk, 585-230-3000).
- **General information** — clinic hours, location, or directions.
- **Acknowledgments** — a thank-you or confirmation needing no clinical action.

### 11.3 Examples of Informational Messages

- "Can I move my appointment from Tuesday to Thursday?"
- "I have new insurance — how do I update it?"
- "Can you send my immunization records to my child's school?"
- "I can't log into the portal on my phone."

### 11.4 Required Handling

Informational messages are routed to the correct non-clinical owner and handled within the Informational timeframe in Part 3. The handling staff confirm there is no clinical content; if any appears — for example, a scheduling message that also reports a new symptom — the message is reclassified by clinical urgency and escalated to clinical staff (the most-urgent-issue rule, Section 7.2).

---

## 12. Decision Rules and Edge Cases

### 12.1 The Triage Decision Sequence

To classify a message, staff apply this sequence:

1. **Scan the full message** for any emergency indicators (Section 8.2). If present or plausibly present, classify **Emergency** and act immediately.
2. If not Emergency, **check for urgent indicators** (Section 9.2). If present or plausibly present, classify **Urgent**.
3. If neither, determine whether the matter is **clinical** (Routine) or purely **administrative** (Informational).
4. For mixed messages, apply the **most-urgent-issue rule** (Section 7.2) and ensure every issue is tracked.
5. When genuinely uncertain between two categories, **classify upward** (Section 7.1) and let a clinician confirm.

### 12.2 Ambiguous or Vague Messages

When a message is too vague to classify safely — for example, "I don't feel right" with no detail — staff do not guess downward. They treat it as at least Urgent pending clarification, attempt to reach the patient promptly for more information, and escalate to a nurse. Vagueness is resolved by contact, not by assuming the benign case.

### 12.3 Historical vs. Active Symptoms

A message that mentions a serious symptom in the **past** ("I had chest pain last year") is not automatically an emergency; what matters is whether the symptom is **active or recent**. Staff read carefully for timing. This distinction is why LRMC uses clinician judgment rather than keyword matching to classify — a historical mention should not manufacture a false emergency, and an active symptom must not be missed. When timing is unclear, staff clarify with the patient and classify upward in the interim.

### 12.4 Behavioral-Health Content

Any message indicating suicidal or homicidal ideation, intent, or a behavioral-health crisis is classified **Emergency** if there is active intent, plan, or an overdose/ingestion, and at least **Urgent** for significant distress without active intent. All such messages are routed under BH-600 to the Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911), and imminent-risk situations follow the emergency escalation in Part 5. Staff never minimize or delay a message expressing thoughts of self-harm.

### 12.5 Pediatric and Proxy Messages

For messages about children or dependent adults sent by a proxy, staff apply the same categories but weigh the lower physiologic reserve of infants and young children — for example, treating signs such as difficulty breathing, persistent vomiting, lethargy, or dehydration in a young child with a lower threshold for Urgent or Emergency classification. Proxy authority is verified under PRIV-400 before clinical information is shared.

### 12.6 Repeated or Escalating Messages

When a patient sends repeated messages about the same unresolved concern, or messages that describe a worsening course, staff treat the pattern itself as a signal to escalate — repetition and worsening both raise urgency. The latest, most severe description governs the category.

## Part 3 of 8 — Response-Time Standards, Coverage, Staffing, and After-Hours Handling

## 13. Response-Time Standards by Category

This Part defines how quickly Lakeside Regional Medical Center responds to portal messages in each urgency category. These are **policy standards**; a departure must be documented with the reason (Part 6). All "business day" measures use LRMC ambulatory business hours (Monday–Friday, 8:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m. Eastern, excluding LRMC-observed holidays). A message received outside business hours is treated as received at the start of the next business day for Routine and Informational standards — but Emergency and Urgent content is acted on whenever it is identified, including the after-hours steps in Section 16.

### 13.1 Emergency

Emergency-category messages are acted upon **immediately upon identification**, without waiting for the next business day. Immediate action means replying with the emergency instruction to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department, attempting direct contact with the patient, and escalating per Part 5. Because the portal is not continuously monitored, the organization's detection controls (Section 15) and after-hours coverage (Section 16) exist specifically to shorten the time to identification.

### 13.2 Urgent

Urgent-category messages receive a clinical response **within 4 hours during business hours**, and the patient is contacted directly within that window when the situation calls for it. Urgent messages identified late in the business day that cannot be safely deferred are handled before close or handed off to after-hours coverage (Section 16) with explicit ownership. Urgent messages are never left to the next business day without a safety plan.

### 13.3 Routine

Routine-category messages receive a response **within 2 business days**. Most are answered sooner. When a routine matter requires provider review that will take longer (for example, a renewal requiring records review), the patient is sent an interim message within the 2-business-day window stating that the request is in progress and when to expect resolution.

### 13.4 Informational

Informational-category messages receive a response or completed action **within 3 business days**. Many are resolved same-day by the appropriate non-clinical owner. If an Informational message turns out to contain clinical content, it is reclassified by clinical urgency immediately and the corresponding faster standard applies.

### 13.5 Interim Acknowledgments

When a message in any clinical category cannot be fully resolved within its standard — for example, because it requires provider review, additional information, or coordination — the owner sends an interim acknowledgment within the standard that tells the patient the request is being worked, what (if anything) is needed from them, and the expected timeframe. Silence is never an acceptable substitute for meeting a standard.

---

## 14. Coverage and Staffing

### 14.1 Daily Queue Management

Each clinic's portal queue is staffed during all business hours, with a designated triage nurse and support staff assigned to monitor and work the queue. Clinic managers ensure coverage is scheduled for every business day, including lunch and break relief, so that no queue goes unmonitored during operational hours.

### 14.2 Out-of-Office and Absence Coverage

When a responsible provider or assigned triage nurse is out of office, their portal messages are reassigned to a covering clinician before the absence begins. Coverage assignment is explicit and documented; messages are never left in the inbox of an absent staff member. The clinic manager is accountable for confirming coverage is in place.

### 14.3 Surge and Backlog Management

When message volume surges or a backlog develops, supervisors reprioritize so that Emergency and Urgent messages are always worked first, redistribute Routine and Informational messages across available staff, and, if needed, bring in additional coverage. Backlogs that threaten the Urgent or Emergency standards are escalated to the clinic manager immediately. Backlog status is reviewed daily (Part 8).

### 14.4 Ownership During Handoffs

Every handoff — shift change, end of business day, coverage transfer — includes explicit transfer of ownership for any open Urgent or Emergency message, with the receiving owner acknowledging the handoff. A message with pending safety-critical action is verbally or electronically signed over, not merely left in a shared queue.

### 14.5 Training and Access Control

Only staff who have completed the required training (Part 8) are granted portal-triage access. Training covers the urgency categories, scope-of-practice limits (Part 4), escalation (Part 5), documentation (Part 6), and privacy (PRIV-400). Access is matched to role, so that, for example, support staff cannot finalize a clinical reply that requires clinician approval.

---

## 15. Detection Controls for Emergency Content

### 15.1 Why Detection Matters

Because portal messages are asynchronous and not continuously monitored, the time between a patient sending an emergency message and a human identifying it is the key safety risk. LRMC reduces this risk with layered detection controls so that possible emergencies surface quickly rather than waiting in a routine queue.

### 15.2 Patient-Facing Prevention

At the point of composing a message, MyLRMC displays the standing notice that the portal is not for emergencies and that the patient should call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department for an emergency. This notice is repeated in the automated acknowledgment. The goal is to divert true emergencies to 911 before they ever enter the queue.

### 15.3 Automated Screening Assistant

LRMC's automated triage-support assistant screens incoming messages for possible emergency content and flags them for prompt human review, proposing an urgency category and a policy-grounded draft reply. The assistant **supports** detection; it does not replace it. The two standing rules always apply: a qualified human reviews and approves any clinically significant reply before it is sent, and the assistant fails safe — a flag raises urgency for human review, and the absence of a flag never lowers the urgency a human would assign. If the assistant is unavailable, the standard human workflow handles all messages with no loss of safety.

### 15.4 Human Confirmation

Triage staff confirm or correct every automated classification. The human triager remains responsible for the final category. When the assistant and the human disagree, the **more urgent** classification governs pending clinician review (classify upward, Section 7.1).

---

## 16. After-Hours and Holiday Handling

### 16.1 What "After-Hours" Means

After-hours is any time outside LRMC ambulatory business hours — nights, weekends, and LRMC-observed holidays. Patients may send portal messages at any time, but Routine and Informational messages received after hours are worked starting the next business day. Emergency and Urgent needs after hours are served by the controls below, not by the routine queue.

### 16.2 The After-Hours Nurse Line

For clinical needs that cannot wait until the next business day, patients are directed to the **after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911**, staffed for telephone triage under PCT-210. The portal's after-hours auto-reply and the standing notices point patients to this line for urgent clinical questions and to 911 for emergencies. The nurse line provides live clinical triage when the portal queue is unmonitored.

### 16.3 After-Hours Auto-Reply

Messages submitted after hours receive an automated reply that (a) confirms receipt, (b) states that the message will be reviewed the next business day, (c) directs the patient to call the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911 for an urgent clinical concern, and (d) directs the patient to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department for an emergency. This reply ensures a patient with a time-sensitive need is pointed to a monitored channel even when the portal is not being watched.

### 16.4 First-Thing Review

At the start of each business day, queues are reviewed promptly for any message that arrived after hours and may contain Emergency or Urgent content, so that such messages are identified and acted upon early rather than waiting behind the day's new volume. Holiday and weekend gaps are covered by this first-thing review on the next operational day, in addition to the nurse line during the gap.

### 16.5 Holiday Coverage

On LRMC-observed holidays, the after-hours nurse line (585-230-0911) and 911 provide clinical coverage, and the portal auto-reply reflects the holiday closure and expected next-business-day handling. Essential clinical services maintain their own coverage arrangements under their departmental policies.

## Part 4 of 8 — Scope of Practice: What Staff May and May Not Advise by Message

## 17. The Scope-of-Practice Principle

Every portal message is answered by someone qualified to answer it. **Scope of practice** is the set of activities a staff member is legally and organizationally authorized to perform based on licensure, certification, training, and LRMC-approved protocols. Answering outside scope — for example, support staff giving medical advice, or a clinician giving advice that requires an in-person exam — creates safety and liability risk. This Part defines what each role may and may not do by message. When a message exceeds the responder's scope, the rule is simple: **route or escalate, do not improvise.**

### 17.1 The Universal Limits

Regardless of role, no staff member uses portal messages to: diagnose a new condition that requires an in-person examination; prescribe or adjust medication outside an approved protocol and prescriber authorization; manage an emergency by message instead of directing the patient to 911; or deliver complex or sensitive results in a way that conflicts with the results-communication rules in Section 20. These limits protect patients from advice that an asynchronous text channel cannot safely support.

---

## 18. What Each Role May Do by Message

### 18.1 Clinical Support Staff (MA/LPN)

Clinical support staff **may**: handle administrative and protocol-eligible messages; relay clearly approved, non-clinical instructions documented by a clinician; gather and document information for the triage nurse or provider (for example, symptom details, vital signs the patient reports, medication lists); and route or escalate anything with clinical content. They **may not**: provide medical advice, interpret symptoms or results, assign Emergency or Urgent categories, or finalize a clinical reply. Potential Emergency or Urgent content is escalated immediately to a nurse (Part 5).

### 18.2 Triage Nurse (RN)

The triage nurse **may**: classify messages by urgency; provide nurse advice within written, approved standing protocols; give general self-care guidance for minor, stable symptoms with appropriate safety-netting; coordinate refills under RX-500 and prescriber authorization; arrange appointments and follow-up; and escalate to the responsible provider. The nurse **may not**: diagnose a new condition requiring examination, prescribe or change medication without prescriber authorization, or exceed the boundaries of an approved protocol. Anything beyond protocol goes to the provider.

### 18.3 Responsible Provider

The responsible provider (physician, NP, PA) **may**: answer messages requiring medical judgment; authorize orders, prescriptions, and refills; interpret and release results consistent with Section 20; and decide whether a message can be safely handled remotely or requires a visit. The provider remains accountable for delegated clinical replies they co-sign. The provider determines when a message cannot be safely resolved by text and must become a visit or a call.

### 18.4 Patient Access / Scheduling and Other Non-Clinical Staff

Non-clinical staff **may** handle scheduling, registration, demographics, records logistics, and general information, and **may not** address clinical content. Any clinical content is reclassified by urgency and routed to clinical staff (the most-urgent-issue rule, Part 2, Section 7.2).

### 18.5 Pharmacy Staff

Pharmacy staff **may** coordinate refills and answer medication-logistics questions under RX-500 and prescriber authorization (Pharmacy, 585-230-2100), and **may not** make prescribing decisions or provide medical advice beyond their scope. Refill clinical decisions belong to the prescriber.

---

## 19. What Staff May Not Advise by Message — Specific Boundaries

### 19.1 No Emergency Management by Message

Staff never attempt to manage a possible emergency through back-and-forth messages. The correct response to emergency content is to direct the patient to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department, attempt direct contact, and escalate (Part 5) — not to advise on home management of a potentially life-threatening problem.

### 19.2 No New Diagnoses Requiring Examination

A new problem that requires an in-person examination to evaluate safely (for example, a new lump, undiagnosed abdominal pain, or a new neurologic symptom) is not diagnosed by message. Staff gather information, provide appropriate safety-netting, and arrange the right level of evaluation.

### 19.3 No Out-of-Protocol Medication Changes

Medication is not started, stopped, or adjusted by message except under an approved protocol with the necessary prescriber authorization (RX-500). Controlled substances are never refilled or adjusted by message outside the strict rules of RX-500 and require prescriber action.

### 19.4 No Advice Beyond the Channel's Reliability

When a question cannot be answered safely without information the message does not contain — for example, an examination finding, a vital sign, or a clarification the patient has not provided — staff do not guess. They request the missing information or convert the message to a call or visit.

### 19.5 Sensitive Topics

Sensitive topics — behavioral health, reproductive health, HIV, substance use, and similar — are handled with the additional privacy care in PRIV-400, shared only with the verified patient or authorized proxy, and routed to the appropriate clinical owner (for behavioral-health crisis, BH-600 and the Behavioral Health Access Line, 585-230-3911).

---

## 20. Communicating Test Results by Message

### 20.1 General Rule

Whether and how a result is communicated by message depends on the nature of the result. **Normal and reassuring results** may be released by message with a brief plain-language explanation and any needed follow-up. **Abnormal, complex, or sensitive results** are communicated by the responsible provider, often by phone or visit, so the patient can ask questions and receive context, consistent with HIM-300.

### 20.2 Significant or Sensitive Results

Results that are clinically significant, that convey a serious or sensitive diagnosis, or that are likely to cause distress are not delivered as a bare value in a text reply. The responsible provider arranges direct communication, ensures the patient understands the result and the plan, and documents the communication. Staff do not pre-empt a provider's planned results conversation by releasing the result first.

### 20.3 "What does my result mean?" Questions

When a patient asks a clinical question about a result, the answer is provided within scope: support staff route it to clinical staff; the triage nurse answers within protocol or routes to the provider; the provider interprets results requiring judgment. A result the patient can already see in the portal still requires a qualified explanation when the patient asks what it means.

### 20.4 Safety-Netting on Results Replies

Results replies that involve any symptom or follow-up include safety-netting — what change would warrant prompt or emergency care and how to seek it — consistent with the safety-netting requirement throughout this manual.

## Part 5 of 8 — Escalation Paths and Safety-Netting

## 21. The Escalation Principle

**Escalation** is moving a message to a higher level of clinical authority or urgency. Escalation is a normal, encouraged part of safe triage, not a sign of failure. The standing rule is that it is always acceptable to escalate and never acceptable to sit on a potentially dangerous message. This Part defines who escalates to whom, how, and how quickly, and defines the safety-netting that accompanies clinical replies.

### 21.1 When to Escalate

Staff escalate whenever: a message contains Emergency or Urgent content; a message exceeds the responder's scope (Part 4); a message is too vague to classify safely; a patient's condition appears to be worsening or the patient sends repeated unresolved messages; or the responder is uncertain. Uncertainty itself is a reason to escalate, not to guess.

---

## 22. Escalation Paths

### 22.1 Support Staff to Triage Nurse

Clinical support staff who encounter any clinical content beyond their scope — and immediately for any possible Emergency or Urgent content — escalate to the triage nurse. The escalation is explicit (the nurse acknowledges ownership) and documented. Support staff do not hold clinical content while deciding what to do; they hand it to a nurse promptly.

### 22.2 Triage Nurse to Responsible Provider

The triage nurse escalates to the responsible provider when a message requires medical judgment beyond approved protocols — a new diagnosis, a medication decision, an abnormal result, or any situation the protocol does not cover. The nurse provides the provider with the relevant information and confirms the provider has accepted ownership.

### 22.3 Emergency Escalation

For Emergency-category content, staff act immediately and in parallel: reply with the instruction to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department now; attempt to reach the patient by phone; notify a clinician (and the responsible provider) without waiting for the patient's reply; and, for behavioral-health emergencies, engage the Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911). The goal is to confirm the patient is getting emergency care, not merely to send a message into an unmonitored channel.

### 22.4 Behavioral-Health Escalation

Messages indicating suicidal or homicidal ideation, intent, or crisis are escalated under BH-600. Active intent, plan, or an overdose/ingestion is an Emergency and is handled with the emergency steps above plus the Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911). Significant distress without active intent is at least Urgent, with prompt clinical contact. Staff never delay or minimize a self-harm message.

### 22.5 Failure-to-Reach and Welfare Escalation

When an Emergency or high-Urgent message cannot be resolved because the patient cannot be reached, staff escalate per the failure-to-reach protocol: repeated contact attempts using all numbers on file, notification of the responsible provider, engagement of emergency resources where the situation warrants (including, in a life-threatening situation where the patient cannot be reached, requesting a welfare check through appropriate channels under clinician direction and Risk Management guidance, 585-230-8100). These steps are documented in full.

### 22.6 Supervisor and Risk Escalation

Operational obstacles to safe handling — backlogs threatening the Urgent or Emergency standards, coverage gaps, or system outages — are escalated to the clinic manager. Privacy, consent, and serious-risk questions are escalated to Compliance / Risk Management (585-230-8100). Technical failures affecting the portal or the triage assistant are escalated to the IT Service Desk (585-230-9000) while messages continue to be handled by the standard human workflow.

---

## 23. Safety-Netting

### 23.1 What Safety-Netting Is and Why It Is Required

**Safety-netting** is explicit guidance telling a patient what symptoms or changes should prompt them to seek more urgent care, and exactly how to do so. Because asynchronous messages cannot monitor a patient over time, safety-netting is the mechanism that protects a patient whose condition may change after the reply is sent. Safety-netting is **required** on every clinical reply that involves a symptom or condition.

### 23.2 Elements of Good Safety-Netting

Effective safety-netting: names the specific warning signs in plain language ("if the pain spreads to your arm or jaw, or you become short of breath"); states the exact action and channel ("call 911," "go to the nearest emergency department," "call the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911"); gives a timeframe where relevant ("if you are not improving in 24 hours"); and is tailored to the patient's situation rather than generic. It always includes the emergency instruction to call 911 for an emergency.

### 23.3 Examples of Safety-Netting Statements

- "If your incision becomes more red, swollen, or painful, you develop a fever over 100.4°F, or you see spreading redness, call us today at [clinic number]. If you have trouble breathing or chest pain, call 911."
- "If you cannot keep any fluids down for more than 24 hours, become dizzy or confused, or stop urinating, contact us right away or call the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911."
- "If you have any thoughts of harming yourself, call 911 or the Behavioral Health Access Line at 585-230-3911, or call or text the national crisis line, right now."

### 23.4 Standing Emergency Instruction

Every clinical reply ends with the standing emergency instruction in plain terms: for a medical emergency, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. This instruction is included even when the message seems routine, because the patient's situation can change between messages.

## Part 6 of 8 — Documentation Standards and the Legal Medical Record

## 24. Why Documentation Matters

A clinically significant portal communication that is not documented in the **legal medical record** is treated, for quality and legal purposes, as if it did not happen. Documentation makes future care informed, demonstrates that the organization handled the message appropriately and on time, and protects both patient and staff. This Part defines what must be documented, how, and when. Documentation follows HIM-300; Health Information Management is reachable at 585-230-4200.

### 24.1 Contemporaneous Documentation

Documentation is contemporaneous — recorded at the time the message is handled, not reconstructed later. Staff document the message, the assessment or action, the advice or information given, and the disposition as they work the message. Late entries and corrections, when unavoidable, follow the late-entry rules in HIM-300 and are clearly labeled as such.

---

## 25. What Must Be Documented

### 25.1 Required Elements for Every Clinically Significant Message

For every message with clinical content, the record includes:

1. **The communication** — that a portal message was received, when, and its clinical substance (the patient's reported symptoms, question, or request).
2. **Identity/proxy verification** — confirmation the sender was the patient or a verified proxy (PRIV-400).
3. **The urgency category assigned** — Emergency, Urgent, Routine, or Informational — and any reclassification.
4. **The assessment** — the clinical reasoning or protocol applied, within the documenter's scope.
5. **The action taken** — advice given, orders or refills authorized, referrals or appointments arranged, escalations made.
6. **Safety-netting** — the specific warning signs and instructions given to the patient.
7. **The disposition and follow-up** — what closes the message and any pending follow-up, with responsible owner and timeframe.
8. **The patient communication** — the content of the reply sent.

### 25.2 Documenting Escalations

When a message is escalated, the record shows who escalated, to whom, when, the reason, and the receiving owner's acceptance. Emergency escalations additionally document the contact attempts, the emergency instruction given, notifications made, and the outcome (for example, that the patient confirmed they were going to the emergency department, or that a welfare check was requested under clinician direction).

### 25.3 Documenting Departures from Standards

When a response-time standard (Part 3) is not met, the record documents the reason, the interim action taken to protect the patient (such as an interim acknowledgment or a safety plan), and the resolution. A missed standard is documented, not hidden.

### 25.4 Documenting the Automated Assistant's Role

When the automated triage-support assistant contributes to handling — for example, by flagging possible emergency content or drafting a reply — the record reflects that a qualified human reviewed and approved the final classification and any clinically significant reply. The human owner remains the documented decision-maker; the assistant's involvement does not transfer accountability.

---

## 26. How to Document

### 26.1 Plain, Objective, Specific

Documentation is written in plain, objective, specific language. Staff record what the patient reported and what was done, avoiding vague entries ("handled message") in favor of specifics ("patient reported 2 days of increasing incision redness and yellow drainage; escalated to provider; same-day visit arranged at 2:30 p.m.; safety-netting provided for fever and spreading redness"). Objective language avoids judgmental characterizations of patients.

### 26.2 Privacy in Documentation

Documentation includes the minimum necessary clinical information and follows PRIV-400 for sensitive categories. Sensitive content is documented in the appropriate, access-controlled part of the record. Staff do not include extraneous sensitive detail beyond what care and safety require.

### 26.3 Linking the Message to the Record

The portal message and its reply are associated with the patient's legal medical record so the communication is retrievable as part of the care history. Administrative-only Informational messages that have no clinical significance are handled per HIM-300's rules on what becomes part of the permanent record; clinically significant messages always do.

### 26.4 Corrections and Addenda

If documentation must be corrected or supplemented after the fact, staff use an addendum or correction per HIM-300, preserving the original entry, clearly marking the change, and recording who made it and when. Records are never altered in a way that obscures the original entry.

## Part 7 of 8 — Reply Templates for Common Message Types

## 27. How to Use These Templates

The templates below are starting points for the most common portal replies. They are written in plain, respectful, patient-facing language and are completed with the specifics of each case. Every clinically significant reply is **reviewed and approved by a qualified staff member before it is sent**; the automated assistant may draft a reply, but a human approves it. Each clinical template includes safety-netting and the standing emergency instruction. Square brackets mark fields to complete. These templates do not replace clinical judgment; staff adapt them to the situation and never use a template to send advice outside their scope (Part 4).

### 27.1 Emergency Redirect (Possible Emergency Identified)

> [Patient first name], your message describes symptoms that may need emergency care right now. **Please call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department immediately.** Do not wait for a reply here. We are also trying to reach you by phone. If you are safe to do so, let us know you received this. For any worsening — trouble breathing, chest pain, severe bleeding, or fainting — call 911 now.

### 27.2 Urgent — Arranging Prompt Evaluation

> [Patient first name], thank you for telling us about [symptom]. This should be looked at promptly. [We would like to see you today at [time]. / A nurse will call you shortly at the number on file.] In the meantime, if [specific red-flag symptoms — e.g., the pain worsens sharply, you develop a high fever, or you have trouble breathing], call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. You can also reach our after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911.

### 27.3 Routine Clinical Question — Self-Care Guidance

> [Patient first name], thanks for your question about [topic]. [Plain-language, in-scope guidance.] This should improve over [timeframe]. If it does not improve, or if you develop [specific warning signs], please contact us at [clinic number] or the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911. For a medical emergency, call 911.

### 27.4 Routine Refill Acknowledgment

> [Patient first name], we received your refill request for [medication]. It has been [sent to your pharmacy / forwarded to your provider for review] and should be ready by [date]. Routine refills are processed within 2 business days, so please request them at least 5 business days before you run out. If you run out of an essential medication or have new symptoms, call the Outpatient Pharmacy at 585-230-2100. For a medical emergency, call 911.

### 27.5 Result — Normal/Reassuring

> [Patient first name], your [test] result is back and looks reassuring. [Brief plain-language explanation and any routine follow-up.] No action is needed right now beyond [next step, if any]. If you have questions, reply here or call us at [clinic number]. For a medical emergency, call 911.

### 27.6 Result — Provider Will Discuss

> [Patient first name], your [test] result is back, and your provider would like to talk with you about it directly to answer your questions. [We will call you at [time]. / Please call [clinic number] to set up a time.] If you have any urgent symptoms in the meantime, call the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911, or call 911 for an emergency.

### 27.7 Request for More Information

> [Patient first name], to help you safely, I need a little more information about [topic]: [specific questions]. Please reply here, or if it is easier, call us at [clinic number]. If your symptoms worsen — [specific red flags] — call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department rather than waiting for a reply.

### 27.8 Scheduling / Administrative

> [Patient first name], thanks for your message. [Scheduling/administrative action — e.g., "Your appointment is moved to [date/time]." / "Please call Central Scheduling at 585-230-2600 to book this."] If you have any health concerns you would like a clinician to review, let us know and we will route them to the right team. For a medical emergency, call 911.

## 28. Template Tone and Safety Rules

Every reply, regardless of template, follows these rules: write in plain, courteous language; tell the patient what was done and what to do next; include safety-netting whenever a symptom or clinical concern is present; offer interpreter support (Interpreter Services, 585-230-6400) when needed; protect privacy and verify the recipient; and always include the standing instruction to call 911 for an emergency. A reply that cannot fully resolve the request says so and gives the patient a clear next step and timeframe.

## Part 8 of 8 — Common Patient Questions (FAQ), Quality Monitoring, and Appendices

## 29. Common Patient Questions

These questions are phrased the way patients ask them, with policy-grounded answers staff can adapt. Each is written to be retrieved and used on its own.

### 29.1 "How fast will someone answer my portal message?"

It depends on what your message is about. Routine clinical questions are answered within 2 business days, and administrative questions within 3 business days. Messages about an urgent problem are handled the same day during business hours. Portal messages are **not** for emergencies and are not monitored around the clock — if you have an emergency, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. For an urgent concern after hours, call our nurse line at 585-230-0911.

### 29.2 "Can I use the portal for an emergency?"

No. MyLRMC messages are not monitored continuously, so they are not safe for emergencies. If you are having a medical emergency — for example, chest pain, trouble breathing, signs of a stroke, severe bleeding, or thoughts of harming yourself — call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department right away. For thoughts of self-harm, you can also call the Behavioral Health Access Line at 585-230-3911.

### 29.3 "What should I do after hours if it can't wait?"

If it is an emergency, call 911. If you have an urgent clinical question that cannot wait until the next business day, call our after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911, where a nurse can help. Routine portal messages sent after hours will be reviewed the next business day.

### 29.4 "Who reads my messages?"

Your messages are read by trained LRMC staff — usually a nurse or your care team — during business hours, and routed to the right person for your concern. Administrative requests go to the appropriate team, such as scheduling or records. A qualified clinician reviews and approves any medical advice before it is sent to you.

### 29.5 "Can I get my test results through the portal?"

Often, yes. Normal and reassuring results may be shared by message with a short explanation. For results that are abnormal, complex, or sensitive, your provider will usually talk with you directly — by phone or at a visit — so you can ask questions and get the full picture. If you see a result in the portal and want to know what it means, just ask and we will have the right person explain it.

### 29.6 "Why didn't I get medical advice for my question — you sent me to a visit?"

Some questions can't be answered safely by message because they need an exam, a test, or more information than a message can provide. When that's the case, we arrange the right kind of visit so you get safe, accurate care rather than a guess. We'll always tell you what to watch for in the meantime and when to seek urgent or emergency care.

### 29.7 "Can my spouse or family member message on my behalf?"

Yes, if they are set up as an authorized proxy on your account, which protects your privacy. To add a proxy, contact the MyLRMC Help Desk at 585-230-3000. We verify proxy authority before sharing any of your health information.

### 29.8 "I sent a message and haven't heard back — what now?"

If you sent a routine message, please allow up to 2 business days (3 for administrative requests). If your concern is urgent or getting worse, don't wait — call us at your clinic or the after-hours nurse line at 585-230-0911. For an emergency, call 911. We monitor for messages that are taking too long, but calling is always faster for something time-sensitive.

### 29.9 "Is messaging private and secure?"

Yes. MyLRMC is a secure, private channel, and we verify we are communicating with you or your authorized proxy before sharing health information. We include only the information needed to answer your question, and sensitive topics get extra privacy protection. Please don't send health information through regular email or text, which are not secure.

### 29.10 "Can I get medical advice for my child through the portal?"

You can message about your child if you are their authorized proxy. We use the same process, but we are extra careful with young children, because they can become more ill quickly. If your child has trouble breathing, won't stop vomiting, is very sleepy or hard to wake, isn't drinking, or seems very ill, call 911 or seek emergency care rather than waiting for a portal reply.

## 30. Quality Monitoring and Oversight

### 30.1 Daily Queue and Aging Audits

Supervisors audit portal queues daily for messages approaching or exceeding their response-time standards and for any ownerless or aging messages, with priority on Emergency and Urgent items. Findings drive same-day reassignment and coverage adjustments so that no safety-critical message is left unworked.

### 30.2 Triage-Accuracy Review

A sample of triaged messages is reviewed periodically to confirm that urgency categories were assigned correctly, that escalations were appropriate and timely, that safety-netting was present on clinical replies, and that documentation met the Part 6 standard. Mis-triage findings are fed back to staff non-punitively and used to improve training and protocols.

### 30.3 Safety-Event Reporting and Review

Mis-triaged messages, missed escalations, delayed responses, and near-misses are reported through the non-punitive safety-event system and reviewed by the PCOC. Serious events are escalated to Compliance / Risk Management (585-230-8100). The organization uses these reviews to strengthen the workflow, not to assign blame.

### 30.4 Oversight of the Automated Assistant

Use of the automated triage-support assistant is monitored to confirm the two standing rules hold: a qualified human reviews and approves every clinically significant reply before it is sent, and the assistant fails safe — it raises urgency for human review and never lowers the urgency a human would assign, and the standard human workflow handles all messages with no loss of safety if the assistant is unavailable. The assistant's flagging accuracy is reviewed so it supports, and never substitutes for, human judgment. Findings are reported to the PCOC.

### 30.5 Metrics

The PCOC reviews metrics including response-time performance by category, triage-accuracy sampling results, escalation timeliness, backlog and aging trends, and safety-event rates. Metrics are used to set staffing and coverage and to identify training needs. No metric is allowed to incentivize downgrading urgency to "clear" a queue; safety always outranks throughput.

## 31. Training and Competency

### 31.1 Required Training Before Access

No staff member is granted portal-triage access until they complete training covering: the four urgency categories and decision rules (Part 2); response-time standards and after-hours handling (Part 3); scope-of-practice limits (Part 4); escalation paths and safety-netting (Part 5); documentation standards (Part 6); reply templates (Part 7); and privacy under PRIV-400. Access is matched to role.

### 31.2 Ongoing Competency

Staff complete periodic refresher training and competency checks, and additional training when policies, protocols, or the triage assistant change. Triage-accuracy review findings (Section 30.2) inform individual and team coaching. Temporary, per-diem, contract, and student personnel complete the same core training before handling messages.

## 32. Appendices

### 32.1 Appendix A — Response-Time Quick Reference

- **Emergency:** act immediately on identification — 911/ED instruction, direct contact, escalation.
- **Urgent:** clinical response within **4 hours** during business hours; direct contact when needed.
- **Routine:** response within **2 business days** (interim acknowledgment if longer).
- **Informational:** response/action within **3 business days**.
- **After-hours urgent clinical:** after-hours nurse line **585-230-0911**.
- **Emergency, any time:** **911** or nearest emergency department.

### 32.2 Appendix B — Urgency Category Quick Reference

- **Emergency** — possible life-/limb-threatening; immediate 911/ED and escalation. E.g., chest pain with arm numbness; stroke signs; anaphylaxis; suicidal intent/overdose.
- **Urgent** — prompt same-day clinical attention. E.g., infected wound with drainage; persistent vomiting; very high blood sugar with symptoms; distress without active intent.
- **Routine** — non-urgent clinical/care matter within business timeframes. E.g., stable refill; reassuring results question; how to take a medication.
- **Informational** — administrative/logistical, no clinical assessment. E.g., reschedule; update insurance; records request; portal login help.

### 32.4 Appendix D — Triage Decision Checklist

1. Read the **entire** message, not just the subject.
2. Verify identity/proxy (PRIV-400) before sharing health information.
3. Scan for **Emergency** indicators → act immediately if present or plausible.
4. If not Emergency, scan for **Urgent** indicators → classify Urgent if present or plausible.
5. Otherwise decide **Routine** (clinical) vs. **Informational** (administrative).
6. For mixed messages, apply the **most-urgent-issue** rule; track every issue.
7. When uncertain, **classify upward** and escalate.
8. Respond within scope (Part 4); escalate anything beyond it (Part 5).
9. Include **safety-netting** and the **911** instruction on clinical replies.
10. **Document** category, assessment, action, safety-netting, and disposition (Part 6).
