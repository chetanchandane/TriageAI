## Part 1 of 8 — Purpose, Scope, Governance, Definitions, and Shared Care-Plan Expectations

## 1. About This Policy

### 1.1 Title and Identification

This is the **Chronic Disease Management Program Policy** of Lakeside Regional Medical Center (LRMC), policy document **CDM-350**. It defines how LRMC supports patients living with common chronic conditions — diabetes, hypertension, asthma and COPD, conditions requiring anticoagulation, heart failure, and chronic mental-health conditions — through structured monitoring schedules, care-plan expectations, medication and laboratory cadence, education, and consistent responses to chronic-care patient messages.

LRMC is a 400-bed community teaching hospital and outpatient network at 1200 Lakeshore Parkway, Rochester, NY 14600. The hospital main line is 585-230-0000. The **Chronic Care Coordination / Care Management office is 585-230-4800**, and condition-specific program contacts are listed in Section 5.1. For any emergency, the instruction throughout this document is to **call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department**.

### 1.2 Why This Policy Exists

Chronic conditions are managed over years through a partnership between the patient and the care team. Good chronic-disease management — regular monitoring, the right medications, timely labs, education, and quick response to warning signs — prevents complications, hospitalizations, and emergencies. This policy exists to:

1. **Standardize good management.** Define monitoring schedules and care-plan expectations so patients receive consistent, evidence-aligned chronic care across LRMC.
2. **Keep patients safely supplied and monitored.** Tie medication refills to appropriate laboratory monitoring and follow-up so therapy is both continuous and safe (coordinated with RX-500).
3. **Catch trouble early.** Give patients and staff clear warning signs and escalation paths so a worsening chronic condition is recognized and acted on before it becomes an emergency.
4. **Support self-management.** Provide education and resources so patients can manage their conditions confidently day to day.

### 1.3 How to Use This Policy

Each Part covers one condition and is self-contained: monitoring schedule, care-plan expectations, refill and lab cadence, education, warning signs, and message-response guidance. Each section can be retrieved and used on its own. Timeframes and cadences here are **policy standards and general targets**; the individual patient's care plan, set by their clinician, always governs the specifics for that patient.

### 1.4 A Safety and Scope Note

This policy is operational guidance, not individualized medical advice, and it does not set medication doses. Targets and schedules are general; a patient's own clinician individualizes them. When a chronic-care message describes a new or worsening symptom, it is triaged for clinical urgency first under **PCT-100** and **EUA-150** — a possible emergency is directed to 911/ED, and an urgent symptom is routed to clinical staff or the after-hours nurse line (585-230-0911) — before routine chronic-care handling. Any clinically significant reply is reviewed and approved by qualified staff before it is sent (PCT-100).

## 2. Scope and Applicability

### 2.1 What This Policy Covers

This policy applies to LRMC's outpatient management of the chronic conditions named above, including monitoring schedules, care-plan documentation, medication-refill and laboratory cadence, patient education, and chronic-care messaging. It applies across primary care and the relevant specialty programs and to the care-management and pharmacy teams that support chronic care.

### 2.2 What This Policy Does Not Cover

This policy does **not** govern: inpatient management; emergency treatment of acute decompensation (see EUA-150); the full detail of every chronic condition LRMC treats (it covers the highest-volume programs); specialty-specific protocols beyond the chronic-care elements here; or detailed prescribing decisions, which belong to the treating clinician. It complements, and does not replace, individual care plans.

### 2.3 Who Must Follow This Policy

This policy applies to primary-care and specialty clinicians managing these conditions; nurses, medical assistants, and care managers supporting chronic care; pharmacists and pharmacy staff coordinating chronic-medication refills and monitoring; dietitians and diabetes/asthma educators; and staff who answer chronic-care patient messages. Only trained staff handle chronic-care messages, and clinical decisions remain with the treating clinician.

## 3. Shared Care-Plan Expectations (All Conditions)

These expectations apply to every chronic condition in this policy. Each Part restates the condition-specific version, but the common framework is:

### 3.1 A Documented, Patient-Centered Care Plan

Every patient with a chronic condition has a documented care plan that includes the diagnosis, current medications, individualized targets (set by the clinician), the monitoring schedule, the follow-up interval, self-management goals, an action plan for warning signs, and the patient's own priorities and preferences. The plan is reviewed and updated at regular visits and after any significant change.

### 3.2 Regular Monitoring and Follow-Up

Each condition has a monitoring schedule (vital signs, labs, and self-monitoring) and a routine follow-up cadence. Refills of chronic medications are tied to appropriate monitoring and follow-up under RX-500: when monitoring or a visit is overdue, staff arrange it and, for essential medications, may provide a bridge supply while the patient catches up rather than interrupting therapy.

### 3.3 Self-Management Support and Education

Patients receive education tailored to their condition and literacy level, including how to take medications, how to self-monitor, lifestyle measures, and what warning signs mean. Education uses plain language and the patient's preferred language (Interpreter & Language Services, 585-230-6400), and reputable resources are offered.

### 3.4 A Warning-Sign Action Plan

Every care plan tells the patient, in plain terms, which changes to watch for, what to do for each level (self-manage, contact the care team, seek urgent care, or call 911), and how to reach help — including the after-hours nurse line (585-230-0911) and the standing instruction to call 911 for an emergency.

### 3.5 Care Coordination

The Chronic Care Coordination / Care Management office (585-230-4800) supports higher-need patients with proactive outreach, help navigating appointments and referrals (SRA-250), medication coordination (RX-500), and connection to community and social resources. Transitions of care (such as after a hospitalization) include prompt follow-up per SRA-250.

### 3.6 Documentation

Chronic-care interactions, monitoring results, care-plan updates, education provided, and message responses are documented in the legal medical record per HIM-300, so the whole team works from a current, shared picture.

## 5. Definitions and Program Contacts

**Care plan.** The documented, individualized plan for managing a patient's chronic condition, including targets, monitoring, follow-up, education, and a warning-sign action plan.

**Monitoring schedule.** The defined cadence of vital signs, laboratory tests, and self-monitoring for a condition.

**Self-monitoring.** Measurements a patient takes at home (for example, blood glucose, blood pressure, weight, or peak flow) and reports to the care team.

**Action plan.** The written plan telling a patient what to do at each level of warning sign, including when to self-manage, contact the team, seek urgent care, or call 911. Asthma and heart-failure action plans are specific, color-coded examples.

**Care manager.** A nurse or coordinator who supports higher-need chronic patients with outreach, navigation, and coordination (585-230-4800).

**Bridge supply.** A limited interim supply of an essential non-controlled medication provided under RX-500 while overdue monitoring or a visit is completed, so therapy is not interrupted.

## Part 2 of 8 — Diabetes Management

## 6. Diabetes — Overview and Care-Plan Expectations

Diabetes management aims to keep blood glucose in an individualized target range, prevent complications, and support the patient's daily self-management. Each patient with diabetes has a documented care plan covering their medications, individualized glucose and A1C targets (set by the clinician), monitoring schedule, follow-up cadence, self-management goals, and a warning-sign action plan. The Diabetes Care & Education Program (585-230-4500) and dietitian services (585-230-4550) support education and self-management.

### 6.1 Care-Plan Expectations

The diabetes care plan includes: current medications (oral, non-insulin injectable, and/or insulin) and how to take them; an individualized A1C target; blood-glucose self-monitoring instructions; nutrition and activity goals; foot and skin care; vaccination status; screening for eye, kidney, nerve, and cardiovascular complications; and a plan for managing low and high blood sugar. The plan is reviewed and updated at each diabetes visit.

## 7. Diabetes — Monitoring Schedule and Lab Cadence

- **A1C (average glucose):** generally checked about every 3 months when above target or recently changed, and about every 6 months when stable and at goal.
- **Home blood-glucose monitoring:** frequency individualized to the treatment; patients on insulin typically monitor more often. The care plan specifies the pattern.
- **Blood pressure:** checked at every visit (diabetes and blood pressure are closely linked).
- **Kidney function and urine protein:** generally checked about yearly.
- **Cholesterol/lipids:** generally checked about yearly or as directed.
- **Eye exam (dilated):** generally yearly to screen for retinopathy.
- **Foot exam:** at routine visits, with a comprehensive check at least yearly.
- **Routine follow-up visits:** generally every 3–6 months depending on control and treatment.

## 8. Diabetes — Refill and Medication Cadence

Diabetes medications are refilled under RX-500, tied to appropriate monitoring. When an A1C or other monitoring is overdue at refill time, staff arrange the lab and, for essential medications such as insulin, may provide a bridge supply so therapy is not interrupted while the patient catches up. Insulin and other essential diabetes medications are treated as continuity-critical (RX-500, Section on essential therapy). Patients are advised to request refills well before running out and to never ration insulin — if they cannot afford or obtain insulin, they should contact the care team or pharmacy (585-230-2100) urgently, because interruptions can be dangerous.

## 9. Diabetes — Education and Resources

Patients are offered diabetes self-management education (585-230-4500) and nutrition counseling (585-230-4550), covering glucose monitoring, medication use, recognizing and treating low blood sugar, sick-day management, foot care, and lifestyle. Education is provided in the patient's language (585-230-6400) and reinforced at visits and through reputable written resources.

## 10. Diabetes — Warning Signs and Action Plan

**Self-manage / contact the team:** Mild low blood sugar (shakiness, sweating, hunger) — treat with fast-acting carbohydrate per your plan and recheck; persistent readings above or below your target range; or symptoms like increased thirst and urination. Contact the diabetes team or care manager for persistent out-of-range readings.

**Seek urgent / same-day care:** Blood sugars that stay very high with symptoms (marked thirst, frequent urination, fatigue); repeated lows; a foot wound or sore that isn't healing; or illness that's making glucose hard to control (sick-day guidance).

**Emergency — call 911 or go to the ED now:** Severe low blood sugar with confusion, seizure, or loss of consciousness (give fast-acting sugar or glucagon if available and trained, then call 911); very high blood sugar with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep/rapid breathing, fruity breath, or confusion (possible diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar state); chest pain or stroke signs; or a rapidly spreading, very painful foot infection with fever.

## 11. Diabetes — Responding to Messages

Triage clinical content first (PCT-100/EUA-150). For routine messages: reinforce the care plan and self-management, arrange overdue labs or visits, and coordinate refills under RX-500. For out-of-range readings without red flags, route to the diabetes team or care manager for adjustment. For any red flag above, direct to emergency care. Always include safety-netting and the 911 instruction. Example reply elements appear in Part 8.

## Part 3 of 8 — Hypertension (High Blood Pressure) Management

## 12. Hypertension — Overview and Care-Plan Expectations

Hypertension management aims to keep blood pressure at an individualized target to reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney disease, and heart failure. Each patient has a care plan with current medications, an individualized blood-pressure target (set by the clinician), a home-monitoring plan, lifestyle goals, monitoring labs, follow-up cadence, and a warning-sign action plan.

### 12.1 Care-Plan Expectations

The plan includes: blood-pressure medications and how to take them; an individualized target; home blood-pressure monitoring technique and schedule; lifestyle measures (reduced sodium, healthy diet, activity, weight, limiting alcohol, not smoking); and screening for related conditions (diabetes, kidney disease, cholesterol). It is reviewed and updated at visits.

## 13. Hypertension — Monitoring Schedule and Lab Cadence

- **Home blood-pressure monitoring:** patients are taught proper technique and asked to record readings on a schedule the plan specifies (often a series of readings over days/weeks when adjusting treatment, then periodic when stable).
- **In-clinic blood pressure:** at every visit.
- **Basic metabolic labs (kidney function, electrolytes):** generally checked periodically, about yearly when stable, and after starting or changing certain blood-pressure medications.
- **Cholesterol/lipids:** generally about yearly or as directed.
- **Routine follow-up visits:** every 3–6 months when stable; more often when adjusting treatment or above target.

## 14. Hypertension — Refill and Medication Cadence

Blood-pressure medications are refilled under RX-500, tied to monitoring. Several blood-pressure medications require periodic kidney-function and electrolyte checks; when those are overdue at refill, staff arrange the lab and may provide a bridge supply of these essential medications so the patient is not left untreated. Patients are advised not to stop blood-pressure medication on their own, since abrupt stopping of some can cause rebound effects, and to request refills before running out.

## 15. Hypertension — Education and Resources

Patients are taught accurate home blood-pressure measurement (proper cuff, seated and rested, recording readings), the importance of taking medication consistently, and lifestyle measures, with dietitian support (585-230-4550) for sodium and weight. Education is provided in the patient's language (585-230-6400).

## 16. Hypertension — Warning Signs and Action Plan

**Self-manage / contact the team:** Readings persistently above your target on home monitoring; missed medication; or mild side effects — contact the care team to review rather than stopping medication on your own.

**Seek urgent / same-day care:** A markedly elevated reading without symptoms that stays high on repeat measurement, per your plan; or new bothersome side effects.

**Emergency — call 911 or go to the ED now:** Very high blood pressure **with** symptoms such as chest pain, severe shortness of breath, severe headache, vision changes, weakness or numbness on one side, trouble speaking, or confusion — this can signal a heart attack, stroke, or hypertensive emergency. Call 911; do not wait on a message.

## 17. Hypertension — Responding to Messages

Triage clinical content first. For routine high readings without symptoms, route to the care team for medication review and reinforce technique and adherence; arrange overdue labs; coordinate refills under RX-500. For very high readings **with** any red-flag symptom, direct to emergency care immediately. Always include safety-netting and the 911 instruction.

## Part 4 of 8 — Asthma and COPD Management

## 18. Asthma/COPD — Overview and Care-Plan Expectations

Asthma and COPD are chronic lung conditions managed to control symptoms, prevent flare-ups (exacerbations), and preserve lung function. Each patient has a care plan including their controller and rescue medications, inhaler technique, an individualized **action plan**, trigger avoidance, and follow-up cadence. The Pulmonary (Asthma/COPD) Clinic is reachable at 585-230-4700.

### 18.1 Care-Plan Expectations and the Action Plan

The plan includes: controller (maintenance) and rescue (quick-relief) medications and correct inhaler technique; a written, often color-coded **action plan** — **Green** (doing well, continue controller), **Yellow** (worsening symptoms or peak-flow drop, step up per plan and use rescue), **Red** (severe symptoms, seek emergency care); trigger identification and avoidance (smoke, allergens, infections); vaccination (influenza, pneumonia, and others as advised); and, for COPD, smoking-cessation support and pulmonary rehabilitation where indicated.

## 19. Asthma/COPD — Monitoring Schedule and Cadence

- **Symptom and control review:** at each visit (frequency of symptoms, rescue-inhaler use, nighttime symptoms, activity limitation, and flare-ups).
- **Peak-flow or lung-function checks:** as specified by the plan; some patients monitor peak flow at home.
- **Inhaler technique review:** at visits and when control worsens (poor technique is a common cause of poor control).
- **Routine follow-up visits:** generally every 3–12 months depending on severity and control; sooner after a flare or hospitalization (post-hospitalization follow-up per SRA-250).

## 20. Asthma/COPD — Refill and Medication Cadence

Controller and rescue inhalers are refilled under RX-500. **Frequent rescue-inhaler refills are a red flag for poor control** and prompt outreach and a control review, not just an automatic refill. Controllers are essential for prevention; patients are advised to keep them filled and to keep a rescue inhaler available and not expired. Refill requests are an opportunity to check technique and control.

## 21. Asthma/COPD — Education and Resources

Patients are taught correct inhaler technique (and spacer use where applicable), the difference between controller and rescue medications, how to use their action plan, trigger avoidance, and — for COPD — smoking cessation and breathing techniques. Education is provided in the patient's language (585-230-6400) and reinforced at visits.

## 22. Asthma/COPD — Warning Signs and Action Plan

**Green (self-manage):** Few or no symptoms, normal activity — continue controller as prescribed.

**Yellow (step up / contact the team):** Increased cough, wheeze, breathlessness, or nighttime symptoms; needing the rescue inhaler more than usual; or a peak-flow drop per your plan — follow the Yellow steps in your action plan and contact the care team if not improving.

**Red — seek emergency care, call 911:** Severe shortness of breath, inability to speak in full sentences, rescue inhaler not helping or wearing off quickly, chest tightness with severe breathing difficulty, or bluish lips. Use the rescue inhaler and call 911. Do not wait on a message for severe breathing trouble.

## 23. Asthma/COPD — Responding to Messages

Triage clinical content first; severe breathing difficulty follows the breathing pathway in EUA-150 (911). For Yellow-zone messages, reinforce the action plan, review technique and triggers, and arrange prompt follow-up; flag frequent rescue use for a control review. Coordinate refills under RX-500. Always include safety-netting and the 911 instruction.

## Part 5 of 8 — Anticoagulation Management

## 24. Anticoagulation — Overview and Care-Plan Expectations

Patients on anticoagulants ("blood thinners") need careful management to balance preventing clots against the risk of bleeding. The LRMC **Anticoagulation Clinic (585-230-2140)** coordinates monitoring and dose management for many of these patients, in partnership with pharmacy (RX-500). Each patient has a care plan with the specific anticoagulant, the indication, the monitoring plan, bleeding precautions, interaction awareness, and a warning-sign action plan.

### 24.1 Care-Plan Expectations

The plan includes: the anticoagulant and how to take it; for warfarin, the individualized INR target range and monitoring schedule; bleeding precautions; awareness of food, medication, and supplement interactions; what to do for a missed dose; and clear instructions on bleeding warning signs. Patients are counseled to tell all their providers and dentists that they take an anticoagulant.

## 25. Anticoagulation — Monitoring Schedule and Cadence

- **Warfarin (INR monitoring):** INR checked on an individualized schedule through the Anticoagulation Clinic (585-230-2140) — more frequently when starting, changing dose, or unstable, and less frequently (often every few weeks) when stable in range. Dose is adjusted by the clinic per protocol.
- **Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs):** do not require routine INR checks, but require periodic kidney and liver function monitoring (generally about yearly, or more often if kidney function changes) and adherence review.
- **Periodic reassessment:** of the ongoing need for anticoagulation and of bleeding risk at routine visits.

## 26. Anticoagulation — Refill and Medication Cadence

Anticoagulants are refilled under RX-500 and tied to monitoring (INR for warfarin; periodic labs for DOACs). **Continuity is critical** — missing doses raises clot risk, and these are treated as essential medications; gaps are avoided and bridge supplies arranged where appropriate while monitoring is completed. The Anticoagulation Clinic and pharmacy coordinate timing so monitoring and refills stay aligned. Patients are advised never to stop or double up on their own and to ask before starting new medications or supplements because of interactions.

## 27. Anticoagulation — Education and Resources

Patients are educated on consistent dosing, the monitoring plan, bleeding precautions (gentle oral care, fall prevention, caution with activities causing injury), interaction awareness (including over-the-counter products and, for warfarin, consistency in vitamin-K-containing foods), and exactly what to do and whom to call for bleeding. Education is in the patient's language (585-230-6400).

## 28. Anticoagulation — Warning Signs and Action Plan

**Contact the team / Anticoagulation Clinic (585-230-2140):** Minor bleeding (small cuts, occasional gum bleeding, easy bruising) that you want reviewed; a missed dose; a planned procedure or new medication; or an INR result you have questions about.

**Seek urgent care:** Bleeding that is harder to stop than usual, a nosebleed that's difficult to control, blood in the urine or stool, or unusual or extensive bruising.

**Emergency — call 911 or go to the ED now:** Severe or uncontrolled bleeding; vomiting or coughing up blood; black or bloody stools with weakness; a severe headache, confusion, weakness/numbness, or trouble speaking (possible bleeding in the brain); a significant head injury or major fall while on an anticoagulant (seek evaluation even if you feel okay); or signs of a clot — sudden leg swelling and pain, or sudden shortness of breath and chest pain.

## 29. Anticoagulation — Responding to Messages

Triage clinical content first; any significant bleeding or head injury on an anticoagulant follows EUA-150 (urgent/emergency). Route INR results and dose questions to the Anticoagulation Clinic (585-230-2140); coordinate refills and monitoring under RX-500; reinforce bleeding precautions and interaction caution. Always include safety-netting and the 911 instruction.

## Part 6 of 8 — Heart Failure Management

## 30. Heart Failure — Overview and Care-Plan Expectations

Heart failure management aims to control symptoms, prevent fluid overload, reduce hospitalizations, and slow progression. The **Heart Failure Program (585-230-4650)**, with cardiology (585-230-4600), supports these patients. Each patient has a care plan with medications, a **daily weight and symptom-monitoring plan**, sodium and fluid guidance, an action plan, and follow-up cadence. Timely post-hospitalization follow-up is emphasized (within 7 days per SRA-250), because the period after a heart-failure hospitalization is high-risk.

### 30.1 Care-Plan Expectations

The plan includes: heart-failure medications and how to take them; daily weight monitoring with a defined "call us" weight-gain threshold; sodium and, where directed, fluid limits; activity guidance; symptom self-monitoring (breathlessness, swelling, fatigue); vaccination; and a clear action plan. Patients are taught that rising weight often signals fluid buildup before they feel worse.

## 31. Heart Failure — Monitoring Schedule and Cadence

- **Daily weight:** at home, same time each day, recorded — a key early warning of fluid retention.
- **Symptom check:** daily awareness of breathlessness (including when lying flat or at night), swelling in legs/abdomen, and fatigue.
- **Blood pressure and heart rate:** as directed by the plan.
- **Kidney function and electrolytes:** monitored periodically, especially with diuretics and certain heart-failure medications, and after dose changes.
- **Routine follow-up:** individualized; close follow-up after any hospitalization or medication change (post-hospitalization within 7 days per SRA-250).

## 32. Heart Failure — Refill and Medication Cadence

Heart-failure medications are refilled under RX-500 and tied to lab monitoring (kidney function and electrolytes for diuretics and certain agents). These are essential medications; continuity is protected and bridge supplies arranged while overdue labs are completed. Patients are advised not to stop heart-failure medications on their own and to keep diuretics and other medications filled, since interruptions can lead to fluid buildup and hospitalization.

## 33. Heart Failure — Education and Resources

Patients are taught daily weight monitoring and the weight threshold that should prompt a call, a low-sodium diet (with dietitian support, 585-230-4550), fluid guidance where applicable, medication adherence, and recognizing early fluid overload. Education is in the patient's language (585-230-6400) and reinforced especially around hospital discharges.

## 34. Heart Failure — Warning Signs and Action Plan

**Green (self-manage):** Stable weight, usual breathing and energy, no increased swelling — continue your plan.

**Yellow (contact the team / Heart Failure Program 585-230-4650):** Weight gain over your plan's threshold (commonly a few pounds in a couple of days); increased swelling in legs, ankles, or abdomen; more breathlessness with activity; needing more pillows to sleep; or increased fatigue — contact the team promptly for adjustment.

**Red — call 911 or go to the ED now:** Severe shortness of breath, breathlessness at rest or that wakes you gasping, chest pain, fainting, a rapid or irregular heartbeat with symptoms, or coughing up pink frothy sputum — these can signal acute heart failure or a heart attack. Call 911; do not wait on a message.

## 35. Heart Failure — Responding to Messages

Triage clinical content first; severe breathlessness or chest pain follows EUA-150 (911). For Yellow-zone weight gain or swelling, route promptly to the Heart Failure Program (585-230-4650) for medication adjustment and reinforce daily weights and sodium guidance; coordinate refills and labs under RX-500. Always include safety-netting and the 911 instruction.

## Part 7 of 8 — Mental-Health (Depression and Anxiety) Management in Chronic Care

## 36. Mental Health — Overview and Care-Plan Expectations

Chronic mental-health conditions such as depression and anxiety are managed in partnership between primary care and behavioral health, with attention to safety, medication, therapy, and follow-up. The **Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911)** connects patients to behavioral-health care and crisis support. Each patient has a care plan with the diagnosis, treatment (medication and/or therapy), follow-up cadence, self-management strategies, and a **safety plan**. Safety is always the first priority (BH-600).

### 36.1 Care-Plan Expectations

The plan includes: the condition and treatment approach (therapy, medication, or both); follow-up and symptom-monitoring schedule; self-management and lifestyle supports (sleep, activity, social connection, reducing alcohol); a clear **safety plan** describing what to do and whom to contact if thoughts of self-harm arise; and connection to behavioral-health services and community resources.

## 37. Mental Health — Monitoring Schedule and Cadence

- **Symptom monitoring:** periodic check-ins using standardized questionnaires and clinical review to track depression/anxiety symptoms and response to treatment.
- **Early medication follow-up:** closer follow-up after starting or changing a medication (commonly within a few weeks) to assess response, side effects, and safety, then periodically when stable.
- **Therapy:** at the frequency set with the behavioral-health provider.
- **Routine follow-up:** individualized; more frequent when symptoms are active, treatment is changing, or risk is elevated.

## 38. Mental Health — Refill and Medication Cadence

Mental-health medications are refilled under RX-500 and treated as essential — abrupt discontinuation of some can cause withdrawal or relapse, so continuity is protected and patients are advised not to stop on their own. Refill contacts are used to check response, side effects, adherence, and safety, and to ensure follow-up is current. Sensitive-medication privacy applies (RX-500, PRIV-400).

## 39. Mental Health — Education and Resources

Patients are educated on their condition and treatment, that medications may take some weeks to work, the importance of continuing therapy and medication as planned, lifestyle supports, and exactly how to use their safety plan. Reputable self-help and crisis resources are provided, including the national crisis line and the Behavioral Health Access Line (585-230-3911). Education is in the patient's language (585-230-6400).

## 40. Mental Health — Warning Signs and Action Plan

**Self-manage / contact the team:** Mild or stable symptoms — continue your plan, use your self-management strategies, and contact the team if symptoms persist or you want to adjust treatment.

**Seek prompt behavioral-health care (585-230-3911):** Worsening depression or anxiety, new or increasing distress, trouble functioning, sleep disruption, medication side effects, or symptoms not improving with treatment.

**Emergency — call 911 or 585-230-3911 now:** Thoughts of harming yourself or others, a plan or intent, having taken steps toward self-harm, an overdose, or feeling unable to stay safe. You can also call or text the national crisis line. This is an emergency and help is available right now — never wait on a message for a safety concern.

## 41. Mental Health — Responding to Messages

Any message indicating suicidal/homicidal ideation, intent, or crisis follows the mental-health-crisis pathway in EUA-150 and BH-600 immediately (911 + 585-230-3911); never minimize or delay. For non-crisis worsening, route promptly to behavioral health (585-230-3911), reinforce the safety plan and self-management, and coordinate medication and follow-up under RX-500. Replies are warm, supportive, and non-judgmental, and always include the safety plan resources and the 911/crisis-line instruction. A qualified human reviews any reply before sending (PCT-100, BH-600).

## Part 8 of 8 — Responding to Chronic-Care Messages, FAQ, and Appendices

## 42. General Rules for Chronic-Care Message Responses

Every chronic-care reply follows these rules, restated here because this section may be read alone:

1. **Triage clinical content first** (PCT-100/EUA-150). A possible emergency goes to 911/ED; an urgent symptom goes to clinical staff or the after-hours nurse line (585-230-0911). Routine chronic-care handling resumes only after urgency is addressed.
2. **Stay within scope** (PCT-100, Part 4). Medication changes and clinical decisions belong to the treating clinician or an approved protocol; support staff route, they don't advise.
3. **Reinforce the care plan.** Restate the relevant self-management step, monitoring, or target from the patient's plan in plain language.
4. **Coordinate medications and labs under RX-500.** Arrange overdue monitoring; protect essential-medication continuity with bridge supplies where appropriate.
5. **Include safety-netting.** Name the warning signs for the condition, what to do, and the 911 instruction.
6. **Document** the interaction per HIM-300.
7. **Human review.** A qualified staff member approves any clinically significant reply before it is sent.

### 42.1 Example Reply — Out-of-Range Home Readings (Non-Emergency)

> [Patient first name], thanks for sending your [blood sugar / blood pressure / weight] readings. I'm sharing these with your care team to review whether any adjustment is needed, and someone will follow up by [timeframe]. In the meantime, please continue your medications as prescribed and [relevant plan step]. If you develop [condition-specific red flags], call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. For an urgent concern after hours, call our nurse line at 585-230-0911.

### 42.2 Example Reply — Chronic-Medication Refill With Overdue Lab

> [Patient first name], we can refill your [medication], and our records show you're due for [lab/monitoring], which helps us keep this medication safe for you. Please [schedule the lab / we've arranged it]. Because this is an important medication, we can provide a short supply so you don't run out while you complete it. Continue taking it as prescribed. If you have [red flags], call 911. (Refill handled under our medication policy.)

### 42.3 Example Reply — Routine Chronic-Care Question

> [Patient first name], good question. [Plain-language, in-scope guidance consistent with your care plan.] Keep up with [monitoring/self-management step], and we'll review this at your next visit on [date] — or sooner if you'd like. If you notice [warning signs], contact us at [clinic number] or the nurse line at 585-230-0911, and call 911 for an emergency.

## 43. Common Patient Questions

### 43.1 "How often do I need lab work for my chronic condition?"

It depends on the condition and how stable you are. For example, diabetes A1C is often checked every 3–6 months; blood-pressure and several other medications need periodic kidney and electrolyte checks (often about yearly when stable); warfarin needs regular INR checks; and heart-failure and other medications need periodic labs. Your care plan lists your schedule. We tie refills to staying current on monitoring so your medications stay safe — if a lab is due, we'll help you get it and can give a short supply so you don't run out.

### 43.2 "Why do I need a visit or lab before my refill?"

Chronic medications work best when we check in regularly — confirming the medication is still right, that monitoring is current, and that you're meeting your goals. When a lab or visit is due, we'll arrange it and, for essential medications, give you a short bridge supply so there's no gap. It's a safety step, not a barrier.

### 43.3 "My home readings are higher (or lower) than usual — what should I do?"

Send them to us so your care team can review and adjust if needed, and keep taking your medications as prescribed unless told otherwise. Don't stop or change doses on your own. If you have warning signs for your condition — for diabetes, severe lows or very high sugars with vomiting; for blood pressure, a very high reading with chest pain, severe headache, or weakness; for heart failure, rapid weight gain or severe breathlessness — seek urgent or emergency care. Call 911 for any emergency.

### 43.4 "Can I manage my condition without coming in so often?"

Often yes, when you're stable — telehealth visits (585-230-3500), home monitoring, and good self-management can reduce in-person visits, and your care manager (585-230-4800) can support you between visits. We still need periodic monitoring to keep things safe. We'll set a follow-up rhythm that fits your control and your life.

### 43.5 "Who do I contact for my specific condition?"

You can always reach your primary-care team, and we have dedicated programs: Diabetes Care & Education (585-230-4500), Heart & Vascular/Cardiology (585-230-4600), Heart Failure (585-230-4650), Pulmonary/Asthma-COPD (585-230-4700), Anticoagulation Clinic (585-230-2140), pharmacy medication review (585-230-2130), and Behavioral Health (585-230-3911). Care Management (585-230-4800) can help you navigate. For an emergency, call 911.

### 43.6 "I take a blood thinner and noticed some bleeding — what should I do?"

Minor bleeding like a small cut or occasional gum bleeding can usually be managed and reported to the Anticoagulation Clinic (585-230-2140) for review. But severe or uncontrolled bleeding, blood in vomit, urine, or stool, a severe headache, or any significant head injury or fall while on a blood thinner is an emergency — call 911 or go to the ED right away, even if you feel okay.

### 43.7 "My controller inhaler is empty but I still have my rescue inhaler — is that okay?"

Your controller (maintenance) inhaler is what prevents flare-ups, so it's important not to go without it — let's get it refilled right away. Relying on your rescue inhaler more than usual is a sign your asthma or COPD may not be well controlled, and we'd like to review your plan. If you ever have severe breathing trouble that your rescue inhaler doesn't relieve, call 911.

## 44. Quality Monitoring and Oversight

The Chronic Disease Management Committee monitors chronic-care quality metrics — for example, the proportion of patients meeting monitoring schedules and individualized targets, post-hospitalization follow-up timeliness, frequent-rescue-inhaler and other early-warning patterns, and care-gap closure — and reviews chronic-care safety events through the non-punitive reporting system, escalating serious events to Compliance / Risk Management (585-230-8100). Metrics are stratified to detect disparities in chronic-care outcomes, and findings drive program and education improvements. Care gaps (overdue labs, missed follow-ups, lapsed essential refills) generate proactive outreach by care management (585-230-4800).

## 45. Training Requirements

Staff managing or messaging about chronic care complete training covering: the shared care-plan framework (Part 1); the condition-specific monitoring schedules, cadences, and warning signs (Parts 2–7); the rule to triage clinical content first and stay within scope (PCT-100/EUA-150); refill-and-monitoring coordination under RX-500; and behavioral-health safety handling under BH-600. Refresher training is completed periodically and when guidelines or programs change.

## 46. Appendices

### 46.1 Appendix A — Monitoring Cadence Quick Reference (general targets; individual plan governs)

- **Diabetes:** A1C every 3–6 months; BP each visit; kidney/lipids ~yearly; eye exam yearly; foot exam yearly; follow-up every 3–6 months.
- **Hypertension:** home BP per plan; BP each visit; kidney/electrolytes ~yearly and after med changes; lipids ~yearly; follow-up every 3–6 months.
- **Asthma/COPD:** control review each visit; inhaler technique at visits/when worsening; follow-up every 3–12 months; sooner after a flare.
- **Anticoagulation:** warfarin INR per clinic schedule (585-230-2140); DOAC kidney/liver ~yearly; reassess need periodically.
- **Heart failure:** daily home weight; kidney/electrolytes periodically and after changes; post-hospitalization follow-up within 7 days.
- **Mental health:** symptom check-ins periodically; closer follow-up after starting/changing medication; therapy per plan.

### 46.2 Appendix B — Condition Red Flags (Call 911 / ED)

- **Diabetes:** severe low (confusion/seizure/unconscious); very high sugar with vomiting, deep/rapid breathing, fruity breath, or confusion.
- **Hypertension:** very high BP with chest pain, severe headache, vision changes, weakness/numbness, trouble speaking, or confusion.
- **Asthma/COPD:** severe shortness of breath, can't speak in sentences, rescue inhaler not helping, blue lips.
- **Anticoagulation:** severe/uncontrolled bleeding; blood in vomit/stool/urine; severe headache or neuro signs; significant head injury/fall.
- **Heart failure:** severe breathlessness or breathlessness at rest/at night, chest pain, fainting, pink frothy sputum.
- **Mental health:** thoughts of self-harm or harming others, intent/plan, overdose, or feeling unable to stay safe (also 585-230-3911).
